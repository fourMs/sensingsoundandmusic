10. Vision

How vision shapes the way we hear sound and music

This week we turn to vision and its role in how we perceive and make sense of sound and music. Visual cues colour how we interpret a sound, set up expectations, and help us follow a complex performance. A musician’s sound-producing actions or a conductor’s gestures influence how you hear rhythm and melody and may change your emotional response. We will look at how the brain combines what we see with what we hear, work through auditory–visual illusions, and consider why visual feedback matters when learning and performing music.

Auditory–visual interaction¶

This chapter first clarifies how vision and audition interact, especially in music psychology and technology, before we turn to the anatomy of the eye and the use of eye tracking in performance and perception research.

Auditory–visual and audio–video¶

In this course we distinguish between the terms auditory–visual and audio–video. They refer to different domains:

  • Audio–video: These terms relate to the technical capture, processing, and reproduction of sound and moving images. Audio refers to the recording, transmission, and playback of sound signals, whether analogue or digital. Video involves the recording, processing, and display of moving images.

  • Auditory–visual: These terms denote the senses and their perceptual processes. The auditory system transduces air-pressure fluctuations (sound) into neural signals that encode pitch, loudness, timbre and spatial position. The visual system transduces visible electromagnetic radiation (light) into neural representations of luminance, colour, form, motion and depth. Perception arises from both the physical properties of stimuli (frequency/wavelength, intensity) and neural processing (adaptation, attention, context), and it underlies how we integrate cues across the senses.

Keeping these apart helps distinguish the physical phenomena (sound and light), how we perceive them (audition and vision), and how we capture them (audio and video). The distinction matters for understanding how audio–video systems work and how we perceive our surroundings, and it matters especially in music, where seeing and hearing combine to shape the experience.

Why “audiovisual” is a tricky word

The everyday word audiovisual takes one half from each pair: audio is the capture word, visual the perception word. It therefore straddles the two domains above. An “audiovisual recording” is a statement about files; “audiovisual integration” is a statement about what a brain does with what the eyes and ears report. The same word can cover a camera setting and a perceptual effect. That is why we try to avoid the term in this book and instead use audio–video for the recording and auditory–visual for the perception.

Integration of the senses¶

Auditory–visual integration is the brain’s ability to combine sound and sight to sharpen perception and comprehension; it underpins tasks like speech perception and shapes multimedia experiences.

Two terms are easy to confuse here. Multimodal perception is the ordinary case: several senses contribute to one percept at the same time. When you watch and hear a drummer, you perceive one stroke, not a sight plus a sound, and each sense adds information the other lacks. The eyes give the preparation and the place of the stroke, the ears its timing and timbre. The senses are combined, and the result is richer than either alone. Crossmodal perception, on the other hand, is when one sense changes what another sense reports: what you see alters what you hear, or what you hear alters what you see, although the information reaching the second sense has not changed.

Multimodal and crossmodal perception are not rival theories but two views of the same machinery. They are both based on sensory integration. A crossmodal effect is what that integration looks like when the senses disagree and the brain settles the disagreement in favour of one of them. The result is often an illusion.

Crossmodal effects range from the bouba–kiki pairing of sounds with shapes met in tuning in to illusions in which one sense overrides another. One famous auditory–visual illusion is the McGurk effect.

When the visible mouth movements of one speech sound are paired with the acoustic signal of another, observers often perceive a third, fused sound (classically: audio “ba” + visual “ga” → perceived “da”). Vision can change what we hear at a basic perceptual level, not only our later judgements.

Dig deeper: what drives and modulates the McGurk effect

The effect arises because the brain combines temporally coincident but conflicting cues from the two senses within a limited time window; cortical areas such as the superior temporal sulcus, a region where auditory and visual information meet, are implicated in resolving the mismatch. Several factors modulate it: timing (asynchrony reduces fusion), signal clarity (degraded audio strengthens visual influence), attention, and individual differences (e.g., lip-reading skill).

The same principles explain why, in music, seeing a performer’s gestures, facial movements, or instrument actions can change the perceived onset, articulation, or expressiveness of a sound. The effect has been shown for singing: when the video of a singer performing one interval was paired with the sound of a different one, viewers heard the interval as smaller or larger depending on what the face was doing Laeng et al., 2021.

Vision is not the only partner sense: touch and vibration also carry musical structure, from bass felt in the chest at a concert to vibrotactile interfaces, and those channels are treated in the body.

Ventriloquism, spatial capture, and temporal binding¶

The ventriloquism effect is named after the stage trick, but in perception research it means something broader. When a sound and a sight arrive close together in time, we hear the sound as coming from where we see its source. In a concert, a film, a game or a VR headset, where a sound seems to come from therefore depends both on the loudspeakers or headphones and on what you see. This is one reason why staging, lighting and picture cuts interact with the mix. The effect is strongest inside a short binding window, from tens to a few hundred milliseconds depending on the task. When sight and sound are clearly out of step, it fades.

The sketch below shows how such binding is usually pictured: two smooth, bell-shaped pulses of onset energy, one auditory and one visual, and a shaded band marking the rough time window within which the brain tends to bind the two into a single event. The widths are illustrative rather than fitted to individual perceivers.

Notation, score reading, and gaze¶

Many performance traditions ask you to split your attention between a score, a conductor, your co-performers and your own instrument. Eye-tracking studies of sight-reading show where the eyes rest. They linger on the places that matter for the structure, such as downbeats, cue notes and page turns, and often return to a spot near a phrase boundary Madell & Hébert, 2008. This connects to the ideas of prediction and subdivision in time and rhythm.

Dig deeper: how firm is the evidence on music reading?

A methodological review of over two decades of music-reading studies shows that tasks, measures, and stimuli vary so much that general conclusions remain tentative Puurtinen, 2018. Studies comparing paper and screen scores suggest differences in scrolling, peripheral preview, and markup, but here too the evidence base is still thin.

Judging with the eyes¶

If vision can change what we hear, it can also change what we think of a performance. The best-known demonstration comes from a series of experiments by Chia-Jung Tsay Tsay, 2013. Participants—musical novices and professional musicians alike—were asked to pick the winner of an international classical music competition from short excerpts of the finalists. Some received sound only, some video only, some both.

The result was the opposite of what almost everyone predicted. Participants given silent video identified the actual winners at rates well above chance. Those given the sound, with or without video, did not. Asked beforehand, more than four out of five participants said that sound was what mattered most to them; their own judgements said otherwise.

A large replication found the same pattern with the original clips but showed that it holds only under limited conditions. With other clips from comparable performances, judgements were at or below chance, and when the performers differed clearly in quality, participants were most accurate when they could hear the music Mehr et al., 2018. What survives is the core point: the visual channel is not decoration on top of an auditory art. It carries information about effort, confidence, expressive intent, and stagecraft, and perceivers use it whether or not they believe they do.

For musicology this cuts in several directions. It bears on how competitions and auditions are run. Orchestras now often run auditions where performers stand behind a screen to remove exactly this kind of bias. It also bears on how we should treat historical accounts of performances that no longer exist except as recordings. And it bears on our own analytic habits. An analysis based on a recording is an analysis of a deliberately reduced version of the event.

The concert as a visual event¶

There are several practical implications of auditory–visual theory. For example, deciding on where performers stand on a stage relative to each other and to the audience sets both what you can see and what you can hear. A soloist stepping forward is a change in visual salience and in direct-to-reverberant ratio at once. Lighting can also be used to directly focus attention. A spotlight is an instruction about where to look, and therefore about which stream to follow.

The increasing use of screens in live concerts attests to the same. Large venues relay close-ups of performers’ hands and faces. At many large concerts, most of the audience watches and listens to mediated images and amplified sound, sometimes arriving from different directions. Livestreamed and recorded concerts go further, since a director’s cuts decide what the viewer attends to, moment by moment, an editorial listening guide that an acoustic concert does not impose.

The same logic shapes music in film, television, and games, where sound and image are constructed together. Michel Chion’s synchresis, met in listening, is the working principle Chion, 2019. Pair a sound with a visible event and the two weld together, whatever the sound’s real source. Foley artists, game audio designers, and composers all rely on it.

Exercise 2 (Where do you look?)

Watch three minutes of an ensemble performance (anything from a jazz trio to an orchestra) and keep a running note of where your eyes go and when.

  • What draws your gaze: the player who is loudest, the one who has just entered, the one moving most, the conductor?
  • Do you look at whoever is playing the melody, or somewhere else?
  • Watch it a second time and deliberately look only at a player who is not leading. Does the music sound different?

The eye¶

This section gives a practical overview of the eye and why it matters for studies of sound, music, and performance.

Anatomy and optics¶

The human eye is an optical organ that focuses light onto the retina, its photosensitive inner surface. The eye adapts to changing light: in bright surroundings the pupil constricts to sharpen the image and reduce optical distortions; in dim light it dilates to maximise the number of photons reaching the retinal photoreceptors.

The cornea, the transparent front surface of the eye, does most of the focusing, while the lens, just behind the pupil, fine-tunes the focus so that images fall sharply on the retina.

The iris, a ring of pigmented muscle tissue, controls the size of the pupil. The pupil’s diameter ranges from about 2 to 8 mm, yet natural light levels can vary by a factor of a million (for example, from moonlight to sunlight).

Figure: The main parts of the human eye.

The retina turns light into neural signals and does some processing of its own, such as sharpening contrast, finding edges and detecting motion. It has three kinds of light sensor. Rods are very sensitive, work in dim light, and see only in shades of grey. Cones work in bright light, give us colour, and are packed into the fovea. A third kind, the light-sensitive ganglion cells (ipRGCs), help set pupil size and the body’s daily rhythm.

Eye movements and the pupil¶

The eyes move in a few basic ways. Saccades are very fast jumps that land the sharp centre of vision (the fovea) on a new spot, with fixations lasting around 200–400 ms. Smooth pursuit lets the eyes follow a moving object when you can see it. Vergence changes the angle between the two eyes so you can focus on near versus far things. In music settings, where someone looks—at a face, the hands, or the score—strongly shapes the visual information they pick up, and that input can change how they hear, understand, or perform the music.

Pupil size is set by the muscles of the iris, under control of the autonomic nervous system. The pupil trades light against sharpness: a small pupil gives a sharper image with more depth of field, a large pupil lets in more light. Pupil size also changes with states that have nothing to do with light, such as mental effort, surprise and emotional arousal. These changes are driven by the brain’s arousal systems, and they can be measured with pupillometry. When reading pupil data, remember that lighting, task difficulty and emotional context all change pupil size.

Eye tracking and pupillometry¶

The eye movements and pupil responses described above can be measured. Two methods do most of the work in music research: eye tracking, which records where the eyes point, and pupillometry, which records how wide the pupils are. Both are cameras pointed at the eyes, and both turn vision into data in the same way that motion capture turns movement into data in the body.

Eye tracking¶

Eye tracking measures where and how the eyes move, which tells us about visual attention, perception, and the cognitive processes behind them. It is used in psychology, neuroscience, marketing, usability studies, and human–computer interaction.

There are two main types of eye tracker: mobile and stationary. Mobile eye trackers are wearable devices, such as glasses, that record eye movements in natural, real-world settings. They suit studies that involve movement, such as sports, navigation, or field research.

Figure: Mobile eye-tracking glasses in use during MusicLab Abels KORK. Image credit: Simen Kjellin/UiO.

Stationary eye trackers are fixed devices, often mounted on a desk or built into a monitor, used in controlled laboratory settings. They suit experiments on reading, visual search, or website usability.

Figure: A stationary eye tracker and its software in use (Photo: MultiLing/UiO).

Gaze tracking¶

Gaze is the direction of a person’s visual attention, and it is our main observable proxy for where cognitive resources go. In music research, gaze reveals what performers and perceivers attend to (hands, face, score, conductor), how they sample information over time, and how visual cues shape what they hear.

Figure: Eye fixations of several individuals (coloured circles) and their durations (circle size). Image credit: Bruno Laeng/UiO.

A tracker records the fixations and saccades described above. Fixation count and duration are common measures of interest or difficulty; saccade amplitude and direction reveal scanning strategies; and the sequence of fixations and saccades, the scanpath, describes a viewing strategy.

Gaze is a useful proxy for attention, but not a perfect one, since people can attend covertly without moving their eyes. Calibration, sampling rate, and tracker type (mobile vs stationary) set the accuracy and decide which metrics are reliable; for precise saccade dynamics you want a high sampling rate (e.g., >250 Hz). Define areas of interest (AOIs) carefully, using dynamic AOIs for moving performers, and account for head motion in mobile settings. Always read gaze patterns against the task demands, the participant’s expertise, and the stimulus timing, for example the anticipatory fixations seen in sight-reading.

Eye-tracking output is a sequence of fixations (where the eyes paused, and for how long) joined by saccades. Below we simulate fixations sweeping across a notional score and show the two standard views: a scanpath (order and dwell time) and a heatmap (where attention accumulated).

Pupillometry¶

Pupillometry is the measurement of pupil size and how it changes over time. Because the pupil responds not only to light but also to cognitive and emotional states, pupillometry lets us study mental effort, arousal, and attention.

Figure: Checking the pupil size before MusicLab Copenhagen.

Decades of research show that pupil diameter tracks mental workload. In a classic study, Hess & Polt (1964) found that the pupil widened step by step as participants solved harder and harder arithmetic problems. Daniel Kahneman, later known for Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), showed that the pupil also widens with each extra digit held in memory, and narrows again as the digits are reported Kahneman & Beatty, 1966. Hundreds of later studies have confirmed this: a larger pupil, or a brief dilation, goes with greater cognitive and attentional demands. Music gives the clearest emotional example. In the chills study described in physiology, the pupil widened at the moments where perceivers reported a shiver, most of all for music they had chosen themselves Laeng et al., 2016. A camera pointed at the eye can thus time an emotion that the person only reports afterwards.

Eye tracking and pupillometry in music research¶

As the equipment has become cheaper and easier to use, eye tracking has spread through music research. Some typical uses:

  • Watching a concert: which gestures and movements the audience looks at, and how what they see shapes how expressive the music seems.
  • Reading music: how musicians’ fixations and saccades across a score differ with expertise and sight-reading skill.
  • Auditory–visual illusions: experiments such as the McGurk effect, where sound and picture disagree and perception follows a mixture of both.
  • Emotion: pupil size as a window on arousal while perceivers hear emotionally charged music.
  • Performance anxiety: how gaze and pupil change under stress on stage, as markers of cognitive load and attention.
  • Interactive installations: where a participant looks controls or triggers the sound.

In all of these, eye tracking and pupillometry show how seeing and hearing work together.

Gaze in performance: conductors and ensembles¶

Performers use their eyes to cue an entry, mark a phrase boundary and stay together. Ensemble players tend to glance up at structural boundaries and risky transitions. A conductor’s gaze spreads attention across the players and signals who comes next. Gaze is part of the visual channel that runs alongside the sound when people make music together, which the body chapter approaches from the side of movement.

Mobile eye trackers take this out of the laboratory. A critical look: do blind people hear better?¶

It is a common belief, reinforced by fiction and by the fame of blind musicians, that losing sight sharpens the ears. This chapter has argued that hearing and seeing are intertwined, so the claim is a natural test case.

  • The claim is that blindness makes the ears more sensitive, and that blind musicians excel because they hear more than the rest of us.
  • The evidence says something more specific. Hearing thresholds are no better in blind people; the ears are the same. What differs is what the brain does with the signal. People blind from early in life locate sounds in the horizontal plane more accurately than sighted people, particularly when listening with one ear Lessard et al., 1998, and they detect changes in pitch faster and more finely, especially when blindness began early Gougoux et al., 2004. Brain imaging shows why: regions that would have processed vision are recruited for hearing, an example of the neuroplasticity that the next chapter describes Voss, 2016.
  • The method compares small groups of early-blind, late-blind, and sighted listeners on specific tasks, which is why the findings are task-specific rather than a general verdict on hearing.
  • The limits are the interesting part. The same early-blind listeners are worse at judging the elevation of a sound source Zwiers et al., 2001, because the cues for up and down are subtle spectral ones that the auditory system normally calibrates against vision. Without sight to teach it, that part of spatial hearing never fully develops. Enhancement in one task and deficit in another is not “better hearing”; it is hearing that has been reorganised.

The popular claim mistakes skill for sensation. Blind musicians play well for the reason sighted ones do, which is thousands of hours of practice, sometimes with attention that vision would otherwise have claimed. What these studies show is subtler and more relevant to this chapter: the senses calibrate each other, and when one is missing the others are re-tuned rather than simply turned up.

Chapter summary

Seeing and hearing are not separate channels. The chapter first set the vocabulary: audio–video for the technical capture of sound and moving images, auditory–visual for the senses and their perception, and the everyday word audiovisual avoided because it straddles the two. Multimodal perception is the ordinary case of several senses building one percept; crossmodal perception is when one sense changes what another reports, as in the McGurk effect and the ventriloquism effect, which hold only inside a short binding window. Vision shapes music beyond illusions: where the eyes rest on a score, how professional musicians and novices alike picked winners from silent video rather than from sound, and how staging, lighting and screens decide what an audience looks at. The eye itself focuses light onto the retina, moves in saccades between fixations, and sets its pupil by light and by the brain’s arousal systems. Eye tracking and pupillometry turn these into data: fixations, saccades, scanpaths and heatmaps say where attention went, and pupil size tracks mental effort and emotion, including the moment of a chill, as long as light, task and context are controlled. In performance, gaze cues entries and keeps ensembles together, and in concert studies the audience’s bodies follow the form of the music. The critical look showed that blind people do not hear more; their ears are the same, and it is the brain that reorganises.
