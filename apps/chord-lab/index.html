How it works

An inversion puts a chord tone other than the root in the bass. A close voicing packs the notes tightly, an open voicing lifts every second note an octave, and a drop-2 voicing takes the second note from the top and drops it an octave, as a jazz pianist or guitarist would.

With smooth voice leading the app tries every way of placing the four voices on chord tones within two octaves, over all inversions, and keeps the one in which the voices move the fewest semitones in total: common tones stay, and the rest move by step where they can. A hollow circle marks a voice that stayed. With no voice leading every chord sits in root position, so the whole chord jumps.

The chromagram is measured from the audio with a spectrum analyser and folded into one octave. The key estimate correlates the remembered chromagram with a major key profile (after Krumhansl) for each of the 12 tonics and names the best match, reading the recent chords as Roman numerals in that key. The harmonics of each tone leak into other pitch classes, mostly the fifth, which is also what a key-finding algorithm has to cope with on a real recording.

Things to try

Play the four triads on C one after the other and hear the difference between major, minor, diminished and augmented. Set a progression that ends on vi instead of I and notice the deceptive cadence. Switch voice leading off and listen to the leaps. Build C13 and change the quality down through C11, C9 and C7 to hear each added third disappear until the plain dominant seventh is back.
