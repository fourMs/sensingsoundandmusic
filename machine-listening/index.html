12. Machine listening

How a computer listens, and how the rest of the book fits together

This is the last chapter, and it looks back as much as forward. Machine listening is how a computer turns sound into information, and building such a listener is a good test of everything in this book: what does a machine need from acoustics, from psychoacoustics, from music theory, before it can say anything about a recording? The chapter follows one idea through five steps. It builds the pipeline on one recording, points the same pipeline at other signals, lets it learn from data, turns it around to generate music, and asks what it learned from and at whose expense. Along the way it collects what the earlier chapters contributed, since a machine listener is built from them. A machine works entirely within the digital representation of the four levels of description; the question to carry through the chapter, and into the exam, is how far up the remaining levels it can be pushed.

What machine listening is¶

Machine listening, also called computer audition, turns sound, whether music, speech or a street, into structured information. The steps are the same every time: capture the audio, extract features that reflect how we hear, find events and boundaries, attach labels, and present the result. Machine listening is the foundation of music information retrieval (MIR), the field that asks musical questions of recordings, and of today’s musical AI. Müller (2021) and Lerch (2022) are the standard textbooks.

The examples below use Python code and the librosa library. You are not expected to learn them, but it helps to know what they are:

From waveform to musical facts¶

The pipeline has four stages: cleaning the audio, extracting features, separating sources, and finding events and sections. Each stage reuses something from an earlier chapter, and the section names it as it goes. The stages are walked through on one recording, and the section ends by asking what the results add up to.

Audio input and preprocessing¶

Audio reaches the machine the way electroacoustics described it: through a microphone, a converter and a sampling rate, as a file of numbers. Before analysis the file is usually tidied. It is resampled to a common rate, centred, normalised in level, and cleaned of hum and clicks, so that recordings become comparable and the later stages behave the same on each.

A machine does not need to see sound to analyse it, but it helps us to remember that it starts where acoustics started: with a waveform, and with the Fourier transform that turns the waveform into a spectrogram. The running example is an excerpt of John Coltrane’s My Favorite Things, whose onsets, beats and swing were discussed in time and rhythm.

Feature extraction¶

Feature extraction turns a waveform or a spectrogram into a few numbers or curves that say something about the sound. The features come in three levels, and each level belongs to a chapter you have read:

  • Low-level features describe timbre and energy from moment to moment: the spectral centroid (the centre of gravity of the spectrum, heard as brightness), spectral flatness (noise against tone), bandwidth, and RMS level. These are the quantities of acoustics and psychoacoustics.
  • Mid-level features describe pitch and harmony: the chromagram, the constant-Q transform, and pitch contours. These are the tools of harmony and melody.
  • High-level features describe time: onset strength, the tempogram, beat tracking, and section boundaries. These belong to time and rhythm.

Skim the names rather than memorising them; the ones used below are explained where they appear. As an example, a chromagram estimates how much energy falls on each of the twelve pitch classes, the same reduction the harmony chapter used by hand:

The instantaneous tempo wobbles, as real performances do, which is why the machine averages before it answers. The estimate of 161 BPM is close to what a listener would say, though we return to it below.

Source separation¶

Source separation isolates individual instruments or voices from a mix. You do this every time you follow one voice in a crowd: it is the auditory scene analysis of the psychoacoustics chapter, and the splitting and merging you heard in the streaming lab. The machine faces the same problem, and it is not solved. A simple version splits the signal into a harmonic and a percussive part:

Signal-processing methods like this are well understood. Deep-learning systems, which have learned what voices, drums and bass sound like from large collections of recordings, do better when many sources overlap. Rule-based methods remain useful when the answer is needed at once or the data are few.

The best systems today split a finished mix into stems: separate tracks for vocals, drums, bass and the rest:

# Source separation: a mix of four synthetic sources, and the four stems a separator tries to recover.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
fs, T = 8000, 2.0
t = np.arange(0, T, 1 / fs)
beat = ((t * 4) % 1 < 0.08).astype(float)                                   # four pulses per second
env = np.convolve(beat, np.exp(-np.arange(0, 0.25, 1 / fs) * 12))[:len(t)]
bass = 0.9 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 55 * t) * env / env.max()

drums = 0.35 * rng.standard_normal(len(t)) * env / env.max()

vocal = 0.7 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (440 + 8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 6 * t)) * t) * (np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.25 * t) ** 2)
stems = [("vocals", vocal, "C3"), ("drums", drums, "C2"), ("bass", bass, "C0"), ("keys", keys, "C1")]
mix = sum(s for _, s, _ in stems)

Separated stems are useful beyond remixing: an isolated drum track makes onset detection and timing analysis far easier, as time and rhythm noted.

Segmentation and event detection¶

Segmentation finds boundaries and events: onsets, beats, the start of a chorus. This is the machine’s version of the grouping problem Bregman posed in psychoacoustics: which parts of a continuous signal belong together? Your auditory system solves it before you notice there was a problem. An algorithm has to be told what counts as a boundary. One classic method compares the recording with itself in a self-similarity matrix, the same picture the video visualiser draws for movement:

Worked example: what can we now say about this track?¶

Each step above produced one number or one picture. On their own they are just outputs. The point of machine listening is what happens when you put them together and ask a musical question of them.

Source
# Pull together what the earlier cells computed, and add a key estimate.
# Krumhansl-Schmuckler: correlate the mean chroma with major/minor key profiles.
major_profile = np.array([6.35, 2.23, 3.48, 2.33, 4.38, 4.09,
                          2.52, 5.19, 2.39, 3.66, 2.29, 2.88])
minor_profile = np.array([6.33, 2.68, 3.52, 5.38, 2.60, 3.53,
                          2.54, 4.75, 3.98, 2.69, 3.34, 3.17])

def best_key(mean_chroma):
    scores = {}
    for shift in range(12):
        rotated = np.roll(mean_chroma, -shift)
        scores[(pitch_names[shift], "major")] = np.corrcoef(rotated, major_profile)[0, 1]
        scores[(pitch_names[shift], "minor")] = np.corrcoef(rotated, minor_profile)[0, 1]
    return max(scores, key=scores.get), max(scores.values())

tempo_bpm = float(tempo_est[0])

Reading the output critically¶

A summary like this is only worth as much as your willingness to check it against your ears. Play the excerpt again and ask three questions.

Is the key estimate meaningful? The chromagram is confident about E, and the key profile agrees on an E centre. That much is fair. But the profiles come from Western common-practice tonality, and they force a choice between two answers, major or minor. Coltrane’s treatment of this tune is modal and moves between minor and major over the same centre, so a single label averages away a contrast that is central to the performance. A tool that can only choose between two categories will always choose between them, however badly they fit.

Is the tempo the tempo? The estimate is a plausible number, but “the tempo” of a piece is not a single physical quantity. Beat trackers routinely lock onto a subdivision or a grouping rather than the level a listener taps, the octave error of doubling or halving the rate. The waltz metre of this tune makes that especially likely. Tap along yourself and compare.

Are these the sections? No. The segmentation returned twenty boundaries because the code asked for twenty (librosa.segment.agglomerative(chroma, 20)). Change that number and you get a different musical form. The algorithm finds points where the chroma texture changes; whether those are musical boundaries is a judgement it never makes.

This is the honest summary of what machine listening gives a musicologist: fast, consistent, reproducible descriptions of acoustic properties, over more music than anyone could listen to. What it does not give is musical interpretation. In the terms of the four levels of description, it is very good at the physical signal and the digital representation, it approximates perception where someone has built a perceptual model into the features, and it does not reach interpretation at all. The features are evidence; the argument is still yours.

The same pipeline, other signals¶

The pipeline above was built for one piece of music, but nothing in it knows what a piece is. Filter, frame, extract a feature, detect an event, count: the body chapter ran exactly this on video, where the video visualiser’s motiongram is a spectrogram of movement and its self-similarity matrix the same picture as the one above; physiology ran it on the heart and the breath, where the camera pulse and the breathing monitor filter a brightness signal and count its peaks. This section points it at two more things: a room, and a whole collection of music. What changes is the question, not the method.

A room¶

ambiscape is a small toolbox for long recordings of places (pip install ambiscape). It turns one idea from the listening chapter into code. MIR attends to the foreground, the notes and the beat, and treats the rest as noise. A soundscape’s subject is the background: the keynote hum, the slow envelope of the day. What counts as noise depends on what counts as signal, as acoustics put it. We synthesise a short room, a ventilation rumble with a door and some footsteps, and run the same kind of features on it:

Source
import os, tempfile

# a low-frequency mechanical "room tone" (fan / ventilation)
b, a = butter(2, 200 / (fs / 2), "low")
rumble = lfilter(b, a, rng.standard_normal(len(t)))
room = 0.3 * rumble / np.max(np.abs(rumble))
# a few broadband events (a door, footsteps)
for onset in (3.0, 8.5, 14.0):

    ev = rng.standard_normal(int(0.15 * fs)) * np.exp(-np.linspace(0, 6, int(0.15 * fs)))
    room[i:i + len(ev)] += 0.4 * ev
room += 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(len(t))            # faint diffuse floor

work = tempfile.mkdtemp()
sf.write(os.path.join(work, "room.wav"), room.astype(np.float32), fs)

The room’s spectral centre sits low and its flatness is small: a tonal rumble, not a hiss, with most of the energy below 250 Hz. The source decomposition is less sure of itself. It scores the recording about as highly for wind as for machinery, which is fair, since low-pass-filtered noise is what both sound like to a feature extractor; a real fan would give itself away by a steady hum at its blade frequency. On a real 27-minute train ride the verdict is clear, with about 93 % of the energy below 250 Hz: an enormous machine that happens to carry people, over which the announcements and conversations are brief figures.

Because rooms are inhabited, ambiscape can export a non-identifying stream of level and spectral features, below the timescale of intelligible speech, and it checks an excerpt for voices before it is shared. Publish features, not recordings, is the principle, and the ethics section returns to it.

Dig deeper: hearing direction with ambisonic recordings

A single microphone captures how much sound there is, but not where it comes from. An ambisonic recording, introduced in electroacoustics, stores the sound field in four channels: an omnidirectional channel W for overall pressure, and three figure-of-eight channels X, Y and Z for the pressure gradient front–back, left–right and up–down. Combining them frame by frame gives an estimate of the direction the energy is flowing from, and of how consistent that direction is. A single talker gives a steady direction; a reverberant murmur gives directions that point everywhere at once. The resulting diffuseness measure separates a room with one talker from a room full of murmur even when their levels and spectra are identical, and it sits naturally beside the level and spectral descriptors above.

Try it

Record 60 seconds of two rooms you know, a kitchen and a bedroom, say, and run the source decomposition on each. Which domain dominates each, and does the number match what you hear?

A collection¶

The pipeline scales once more, from a piece to a room to a whole library. musiscape (pip install musiscape) takes a folder of albums and draws how the albums differ, which tracks resemble which, how consistent each album is, and where the outliers sit; its documentation has a gallery of the plots. musiscape and ambiscape are siblings and share their machinery, since a tonal centre is a tonal centre whether it describes an album or an afternoon in a courtyard.

What changes across the three scales is not the method. At every scale the pipeline is still waveform, framing, features, decision. What changes is the question you can sensibly ask of the output: notes and phrases from a piece, states and rhythms measured in minutes from a room, similarity and grouping from a collection. Ask a collection-scale question of a single recording and you will get an answer, and it will not mean anything.

Learning to listen¶

So far the machine has only described. The next step is to let it decide: is this a trumpet, which genre, which song, will this listener like it? These are the tasks of music information retrieval (MIR), machine listening aimed at musical questions, and most of them are answered today by systems that learned the answer from examples rather than being told the rules. The main tasks first, then how the learning works.

  • Classification and recognition: which instrument, which genre, speech or music. The pitfalls are biased datasets and instruments that sound alike.
  • Recommendation: audio features combined with listening behaviour. Useful for discovery, prone to bias, and hungry for personal data.
  • Transcription: audio into notes. Single melodies work well; full polyphonic transcription does not yet.
  • Structure analysis: verses, choruses, boundaries. “Meaningful” is subjective, so evaluation needs human judgement.
  • Search by description: “find tracks with a trumpet solo”. The hard part is mapping everyday words to reliable labels.
  • Mood and emotion: estimating how music feels. This is where MIR meets the music psychology of physiology, with all the individual and cultural differences that chapter described.

From rules to learning¶

Two kinds of system answer these questions. Rule-based systems use hand-written signal processing: thresholds, peak picking, decision rules. They are small, fast, easy to understand and easy to tune, and they break when the music is unlike what they were tuned on. Learning-based systems find their own patterns in data. They are usually more accurate and more adaptable, and they need more data and computation, are harder to interpret, and inherit the biases of their training sets. Many systems are hybrids: a simple rule proposes, a learned model refines. You will meet the names of several model families in the literature; the dropdown says what each is good at.

Dig deeper: model families

  • Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) describe a signal as moving between a small set of hidden states, such as notes or chords, and estimate the most likely state sequence. They are lightweight and work with little data, but struggle when musical patterns stretch over long spans.

  • Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks process a sequence one step at a time, with LSTMs adding an internal memory for longer context. They handle moderate musical context well but are slower than models that process a whole sequence at once.

  • Temporal or dilated convolutional networks (temporal CNNs, TCNs) apply the pattern-matching filters of a convolutional neural network along the time axis, with deeper layers seeing longer stretches of signal. They compute efficiently and suit low-latency streaming, but the amount of context they see is fixed by the design.

  • Transformers and attention-based models let every moment in a sequence relate directly to every other, which suits long-range structure and tasks such as transcription and form analysis. They often give the best results but demand far more computation and memory, especially for long recordings.

Whichever kind of system, the machine borrows from the listener. The mel scale, loudness models and masking curves of psychoacoustics decide how features weight the signal. The window and hop sizes that set a system’s time resolution follow from how long a listener integrates sound. And the labels that learning systems are trained on, key, genre, mood, groove, are perceptual categories, which is why datasets need human annotators and results need listening tests. In return, technology gives music psychology its stimuli, its sensors and its models: every measuring device in chapters 8 to 11 is a machine listening to a body.

Artificial neural networks¶

Most learning-based systems today are built from artificial neural networks (ANNs). The brain explained where the metaphor comes from and how far it stretches; here is the mechanism. A network is a set of simple units arranged in layers. Each unit multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up, passes the sum through a simple non-linear function, and hands the result to the next layer.

A simple artificial neural network with an input layer, one hidden layer, and an output layer.

Training means showing the network many examples, measuring how wrong its output is, and nudging every weight a little towards being less wrong, a few million times over. Deep learning stacks many layers, so that early layers pick up local detail (a transient, a spectral edge) and later layers combine it into something abstract (a timbre, a chord, a genre). Two consequences follow. The features are no longer chosen by you but found by the network, which is what makes these systems powerful and also hard to interrogate. And whatever the training data contained is what the network learned, which is why the ethics section below is about datasets as much as about models.

Real time or offline¶

Whether a system is rule-based or learned, time divides it once more. A real-time system must answer while the music plays, as in live accompaniment or an interactive installation. It hears only what has arrived and has a few tens of milliseconds to decide, so it has to be simple and quick. An offline system has the whole recording and all the time it likes. It can look ahead, revise earlier decisions and run expensive smoothing, which is why transcription and structural analysis are done offline. Musical structure is easiest to see once you know how it turned out, and a live system never has that luxury. The window explorer app shows where the latency comes from.

Dig deeper: where the latency comes from

Latency ≈ window duration + model lookahead + processing time. With n_fft=2048 at sr=22050, the analysis window alone is ≈ 92.9 ms, and a hop_length of 512 advances in 23.2 ms steps, so even before any model runs, the system is already that far behind the sound.

Real-time work therefore requires causal algorithms (causal filters, causal convolutions, stateful RNNs/TCNs, or streaming transformers with limited lookahead), a streaming frontend with short windows and small hops, cheap incremental features (onset strength, low-band log-mel, incremental CQT), and simple online post-processing (peak picking, adaptive thresholds). Compute has to be bounded and predictable to meet deployment constraints (CPU/GPU, battery, I/O), which in practice means lightweight models or hybrid pipelines with a rule-based front end and a small learned model.

Offline systems can instead use larger windows, global context, and bidirectional models (bi-RNNs, full attention), plus expensive post-processing (Viterbi smoothing, global segmentation, search) and whole-track normalisation.

Turning the pipeline around: generative music AI¶

Everything so far has been analysis: extracting information from music that already exists, or deciding something about it. Generative music AI turns the pipeline around and produces new music Briot et al., 2020. The two directions are close relatives, since a system can only generate convincingly in a style it has learned to represent, and generative models reuse the model families described above.

Generation comes in two flavours. Symbolic generation works with notes. The folk-tune generator FolkRNN from harmony and melody is an example: trained on thousands of transcribed Irish tunes, it writes new melodies in notation that a musician can play, edit or discard, and it is the melody lab’s next-note prediction scaled up. Audio generation skips the score and produces a finished recording. Research systems such as MusicLM and MusicGen showed that a written prompt like “upbeat funk with slap bass” could become sound, and commercial services such as Suno and Udio now deliver complete songs with vocals from a sentence of text.

How do such systems learn? They process huge collections of music, adjusting their weights until they can predict how such music tends to continue. When prompted, they assemble new output that follows the statistical patterns they have absorbed. They do not replay any single piece from the training set.

The training set is where the debate starts. Much of the music these systems learned from is copyrighted, and the artists who made it were generally not asked, credited or paid Sturm et al., 2019. In 2024 major record labels took leading text-to-music services to court over the use of their catalogues, and that litigation is still running. There is a cultural worry too: models trained mostly on commercially successful Western repertoire pull their output towards that average, and a flood of generated music could crowd out the diversity the models learned from.

What are these systems good at? Today they excel at idiomatic pastiche: a plausible thirty-second lo-fi beat, a jingle in the style of 1980s synth-pop. They are much weaker at long-range form, at developing an idea over several minutes, and at the deliberate rule-breaking through which styles change. Many artists therefore treat them as tools rather than replacements, generating raw material to sample and rework, much as samplers and drum machines were absorbed into practice after controversies of their own.

What the machine learned from: data and ethics¶

Music data¶

Every system in this chapter is only as good as the music it learned from, which makes the data the place where the technical questions become ethical ones. MIR used to work mostly with symbolic data, MIDI and MusicXML files. Most research now works with audio, which carries no symbols at all. Around the sound sit metadata (artist, album), paradata (how it was recorded or analysed) and user data (who listened, and when). Getting diverse music data while respecting copyright, performers’ rights and privacy is one of the field’s standing problems. Researchers therefore lean on openly licensed recordings and documented datasets, and share features or spectrograms when the audio itself cannot be shared.

Combining data types¶

Many systems add other data to the audio: lyrics to settle a mood, a score for exact pitches, video for the performer’s gestures. The MIR literature calls this multimodal MIR, borrowing the word that vision uses for perception through several senses. This book keeps multimodal for perception and speaks of data types here: a score and a recording are two kinds of data about one piece, not two senses.

Question

Why is “multimodal data” a challenging term when working between music psychology and technology?

Two problems recur. The first is alignment, since audio, scores, lyrics and video run on different clocks. The second is comparison between kinds of data, which many systems solve with embeddings: lists of numbers from a trained model that place similar items, whether clips, lyric lines or images, close together.

Ethics¶

The datasets and the models built on them have social consequences. Whenever you use features and classifiers, keep these issues in view:

  • Consent and privacy: field recordings, concerts and classroom recordings contain identifiable voices. Record consent, licences and known limitations with every dataset, and share features rather than audio where you can.
  • Bias: training data skewed by genre, language, recording quality or Western labels give models that fail silently on the music they never saw.
  • Training data and generation: the generative systems above raise unresolved questions of artist consent, credit and compensation.
  • Surveillance: mood detection, speaker identification and copyright enforcement can be misused. Ask where and on whom a system will be deployed.
  • Environmental cost: large models and long GPU jobs use energy. Keep the method proportionate to the question.
  • Cultural scope: perception and meaning are culturally conditioned, so a model trained on a narrow corpus misrepresents the practices outside it. Use diverse styles and real recordings, not only studio productions.
  • Clinical claims: music-based therapy tools must be validated in proper trials before any benefit is claimed, as the brain chapter’s music-therapy section showed.
  • Transparency: when AI assists your writing or analysis, disclose it where the rules require and keep responsibility for the claims you publish.

These points do not forbid computational work. They are part of doing it well.

Open-source libraries

  • librosa: A lightweight, high‑level Python library for audio analysis and feature extraction (spectrograms, chroma, MFCCs, tempograms). Good for prototyping and education. (pip install librosa)

  • Essentia: C++ DSP library with Python bindings: very large feature set, audio descriptors, and production‑grade implementations of many MIR algorithms (beat, onset, pitch, timbre). Useful when performance and completeness matter.

  • madmom: Focused on MIR tasks such as beat tracking, onset detection, and tempo estimation with efficient Cython code and pretrained models. Good for beat/onset pipelines and real-time algorithms.

  • Spleeter (Deezer): Fast, easy-to-use source separation (2/4/5 stems) with pretrained U‑Net models. Great for quick vocal/instrument separation and dataset preparation.

  • Open‑Unmix (UMX): Open, research‑oriented music source separation model (frequency‑domain, PyTorch) and evaluation scripts (museval). Good for reproducible separation experiments.

  • Demucs: Time‑domain neural separator (waveform) that often yields strong perceptual quality for music separation. Useful as a modern baseline for source separation work.

  • CREPE: A convolutional model for pitch/F0 estimation directly from waveform. Provides frame‑level pitch estimates and confidence measures.

  • Onsets & Frames (Magenta): Neural system for piano transcription (onset detection + framewise prediction + decoding). Good example of end‑to‑end transcription pipelines.

  • mir_eval / museval: Lightweight toolkits for standard MIR evaluation metrics. Use these for consistent, comparable results.

  • JAMS: JSON‑based format and Python tools for storing and exchanging musical annotations (beats, chords, segments). Useful for reproducible annotation workflows.

Datasets and benchmarks

  • MUSDB18: Standard dataset for music source separation (stereo mixtures + stems).

  • MAESTRO: Aligned piano audio + MIDI dataset used for transcription and generation.

  • GTZAN / Million Song Dataset: Popular collections for genre / large‑scale analysis (beware of known issues and biases).

Commercial services

  • Shazam — A consumer-facing music recognition service that identifies songs from short audio clips and returns metadata (title, artist, album) and listening links. Widely integrated into iOS/Android and many commercial platforms.

  • ACRCloud — Commercial audio fingerprinting and automatic content recognition (ACR) for music identification, broadcast monitoring, copyright compliance, and synchronised second‑screen experiences. Offers cloud APIs and on‑device SDKs for streaming, broadcasting, and automotive use cases.

  • Gracenote — Metadata and music‑recognition services powering search, recommendation, and interactive media features for streaming platforms, consumer electronics, and infotainment systems. Commonly used to enrich catalogues and enable content discovery.

  • Audible Magic — Content identification and rights management platform specialising in copyright compliance, fingerprinting, and monetisation for user‑generated and professional video/audio content. Used by platforms that need automated rights enforcement and revenue attribution.

  • Samba TV — A television analytics company that uses automatic content recognition (ACR) to identify what is playing on screen, powering audience measurement, personalised advertising, and interactive TV experiences.

A critical look: does all music sound the same these days?¶

Every generation says it about the next one’s music, and in 2012 it became a headline with a study behind it: science had shown that pop music was getting louder, blander and more alike. The evidence came from exactly the tools of this chapter, audio features computed over hundreds of thousands of recordings, so it is a good last case for asking what such tools can and cannot settle.

  • The claim is that popular music has become louder and less varied over the decades, in melody, harmony, and timbre.
  • The evidence is genuinely mixed. An analysis of nearly half a million tracks from 1955 to 2010 found that loudness rose steadily, that the range of timbres in use narrowed, and that transitions between pitches became more restricted over time Serrà et al., 2012. A later study of seventeen thousand songs from the US charts, 1960 to 2010, described the same period differently: it found no steady decline in diversity but three stylistic revolutions, in 1964, 1983, and 1991, with a dip in diversity in the mid-1980s, when drum machines and synthesisers dominated, followed by a recovery as hip-hop arrived Mauch et al., 2015.
  • The method is the same in both, chroma, timbre, and loudness features extracted from audio and tracked over time, so the disagreement is instructive. It comes from which recordings were included, which features were chosen, and what “diversity” is taken to mean, and each of those is a decision made by the analyst, not a fact found in the data.
  • The limits apply to any such claim. Descriptors are proxies for musical qualities, not the qualities themselves. Datasets over-represent charts and Western pop. And the sample of old music we compare against has been filtered by decades of forgetting, so the past always sounds more varied than it was.

One part of the claim is robust and measurable: recordings have become louder, the loudness war that the psychoacoustics chapter’s loudness standards were written to end. The rest, that music got worse, is not something a feature extractor can answer. Machine listening can tell you what changed in the audio; whether the change is a loss is a judgement the listener still has to make.

Chapter summary

Machine listening treats audio as data and runs one pipeline on it: clean the signal, extract features, separate sources, find events and sections, then ask a musical question of the result. Each stage borrows from an earlier chapter, from the spectrogram of acoustics to the auditory scene analysis of psychoacoustics, the beat of time and rhythm and the chromagram of harmony and melody. The same pipeline serves video, body signals, rooms and whole collections; what changes with the signal is the question, not the method. Music information retrieval lets the machine decide rather than describe, mostly with systems that learned from examples, under the constraints of real time or offline; generative AI turns the pipeline around; and every one of these systems is only as good, and as fair, as the data it learned from. In the four levels of description the machine is strong at the signal and its digital representation, approximates perception where a perceptual model has been built in, and does not reach interpretation. The closing case showed that large-scale audio analysis confirms rising loudness but cannot settle whether music got worse: what changed is measurable, whether it is a loss is a judgement for the perceiver.
