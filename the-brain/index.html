11. The brain

How the brain processes sound and music

Everything we have studied so far ends up in the brain. It is also the place where the chain folds back: expectation and attention change what the auditory cortex does to a signal. The cochlea and psychoacoustics, embodiment, and physiological reactions all feed a brain that turns vibrating air into pitch, rhythm, timbre, emotion, and memory. The signal is reshaped at every step as it travels from the ear to the cortex and the limbic system. Harmony and melody described pitch and tonal structure in musical terms. This week looks at which brain regions are doing the work, how they cooperate, and how researchers measure their activity while we listen and perform.

The human brain¶

Some 86 billion neurons, and about as many supporting glial cells Azevedo et al., 2009, make the brain the most intricate organ we have. It is where all sensory input is gathered and where perception, cognition, emotion, and movement are coordinated. The brain is also remarkably plastic. It reorganises itself in response to musical training, sensory experience, and even injury. That is why we can pick up new musical skills, adapt after hearing loss, and grow particular musical tastes. By weaving together signals from the outside world and from our own internal states, it lets us interpret sound and music and find meaning in them. Its main anatomical regions include:

  • Frontal lobe: At the front of the brain, this region handles executive functions—decision-making, planning, problem-solving, voluntary movement—and shapes aspects of personality and social behaviour.

  • Parietal lobe: Sitting behind the frontal lobe, it processes bodily sensation: touch, temperature, pain, and spatial orientation.

  • Temporal lobe: Tucked beneath the frontal and parietal lobes, it carries much of our auditory processing, language comprehension, and memory formation.

  • Occipital lobe: At the back of the brain, this region is devoted mainly to vision.

  • Cerebellum: Below the occipital lobe, it coordinates voluntary movement, balance, posture, and motor learning.

  • Cingulate gyrus: A part of the limbic system, it contributes to emotion, learning, and memory, and ties behavioural outcomes to motivation.

Illustration of the brain’s lobes (Wikimedia Commons).

Note

The terms brain and mind are often used interchangeably, but they refer to different things. The brain is a physical organ made up of billions of neurons and supporting cells. The mind is the set of mental processes and experiences that arise from brain activity: thoughts, emotions, memories, consciousness, and self-awareness.

Neurons¶

Neurons are the cells that carry signals in the brain and the rest of the nervous system. They do so with electricity inside the cell and with chemistry between cells. A neuron has three main parts. The cell body, or soma, holds the nucleus. The dendrites are branches that receive signals from other neurons. The axon is a long fibre that carries the neuron’s own signal away, to other neurons, to muscles, or to glands.

Illustration of a multipolar neuron (Wikimedia Commons).

Neurons do not touch. Where an axon meets the next dendrite there is a tiny gap, the synapse. The signal crosses it as a chemical: the axon releases neurotransmitters, and the dendrite on the other side picks them up. A neuron adds up everything its dendrites receive. If the sum is large enough, it fires an action potential, a brief electrical pulse that runs down the axon to the next synapse. Billions of cells doing this, many times a second, is what hears a melody, moves a hand, and remembers a song.

Dig deeper: artificial neural networks

The brain has also served as a design metaphor. Artificial neural networks (ANNs), the machinery behind most of what is now called artificial intelligence, take their name and their basic picture from the cells described above: many simple units, each summing its inputs and passing on a result, wired together in layers whose connection strengths change with use.

The analogy is worth holding loosely. A network’s “learning” is the adjustment of numerical weights to reduce an error, which is a thin cartoon of synaptic plasticity, and its units have none of the chemistry, timing, or anatomy of a neuron. The traffic between the two fields has nonetheless run both ways. Neuroscience lent machine learning a metaphor, and trained networks are now used in return as explicit hypotheses about what the auditory system might be computing, to be tested against recordings from real brains. Machine listening takes the topic up on the technical side.

The listening brain¶

Hearing begins in the ear, but listening is done by the brain. The regions and pathways below are the ones that turn the signal from the cochlea into a heard sound, and then into a melody, a beat, a memory, and a feeling.

Key brain regions for listening¶

A handful of regions do much of the work when we process sound and music:

  • The auditory cortex, in the temporal lobe, decodes basic sound features such as pitch, loudness, and timbre, and is central to recognising and interpreting musical elements.
  • The prefrontal cortex handles higher-order work: directing attention, spotting patterns, and predicting where the music is going.
  • Motor areas respond even when we only listen, reflecting how the brain responds to rhythm and beat and prepares for movement Zatorre et al., 2007. A steady beat engages above all the premotor cortex, the supplementary motor area and the basal ganglia, a group of structures deep in the brain that also time our own movements Grahn & Brett, 2007.
  • The limbic system, which includes the amygdala and hippocampus, drives emotional responses to music and ties pieces to memories.
  • The nucleus accumbens, part of the reward system, is linked to the pleasure and motivation that music can stir up Salimpoor et al., 2011.

Auditory pathways¶

Psychoacoustics followed sound as far as the cochlea, where hair cells turn vibration into nerve impulses, ordered by frequency. This chapter picks the signal up from there. The auditory nerve carries it to the brainstem. The first stop is the cochlear nuclei. From there the signal climbs through a chain of relay stations in the brainstem, the midbrain and the thalamus before it reaches the cortex.

Dig deeper: the relay stations of the auditory pathway
  • The superior olivary complex, in the brainstem, compares the timing and intensity of sound at the two ears, which is the basis for locating sounds in space.
  • The inferior colliculus, in the midbrain, gathers auditory information and supports reflexive responses such as turning the head towards a noise.
  • The medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus works as a relay hub, routing auditory signals on to the right parts of the cerebral cortex.

The last stop is the primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. Here the basic features are read out: pitch, loudness and timbre. Neighbouring areas then take over and recognise larger things, such as a voice, a word, or a tune. Speech and music arrive through the same pathway and are handled by overlapping areas, which raises an old question: how much do the two share? Aniruddh Patel’s answer, in Music, Language, and the Brain, is that they store different things, words in one place and chords in another, but use the same machinery to put a sequence together Patel, 2007. A wrong chord and an ungrammatical word disturb overlapping brain responses, and someone with a damaged sense of syntax in one domain often has it in the other. That is why the brain chapter keeps returning to expectation: the same prediction machinery runs under both.

Two things make the pathway powerful. It is a hierarchy, so each stage builds on the one below. It is also parallel, so several features of a sound are analysed at the same time. That is why we can hear where a sound comes from, what made it, and what it means, all at once.

Neural processing of sound¶

In the auditory cortex, different groups of neurons respond to different features of the sound:

  • Frequency: Neurons are laid out by the frequency they respond to, low at one end and high at the other, a tonotopic map like the one in the cochlea. Pitch is not read straight off this map. It is computed from the pattern across it, which is why we hear a missing fundamental (psychoacoustics).
  • Loudness: A louder sound makes the neurons fire faster.
  • Timbre: The pattern across many frequencies at once tells one instrument or voice from another.
  • Rhythm and timing: Patterns in time are tracked by the auditory cortex together with motor areas, which is why we can find and follow a beat.
Dig deeper: lateralisation

Lateralisation describes how some auditory functions lean more on one hemisphere than the other. The left hemisphere tends to specialise in rapid temporal changes, like those in speech and rhythm, which makes it important for language comprehension and rhythmic analysis. The right hemisphere is generally more sensitive to fine spectral, frequency-based information, and so matters more for melody and pitch Zatorre et al., 2002. The difference is one of degree: both hemispheres take part in all of these tasks. Splitting the work this way lets the brain analyse complex sounds like music by handling different features in parallel.

Music cognition¶

Music cognition is the mental work of making sense of music. Listening goes far beyond analysing sound: we recognise a melody, expect the next chord, feel the rhythm, and remember where we heard it. None of this happens in one place. The features from the auditory cortex are combined with what the regions listed above contribute: the prefrontal cortex adds patterns, expectation and working memory, motor areas the beat, the limbic system feeling and memories from our own life, and the reward system the moments we find pleasurable. This is where a sound becomes a familiar tune, where we sense where it is going, and where it links to our past. How they cooperate in detail is still an open research question.

Mirror neurons¶

Mirror neurons are a special class of cell that fire both when you perform an action and when you watch someone else perform the same one. First found in the premotor cortex of monkeys, they are thought, on mostly indirect evidence in humans, to matter for understanding actions, imitation, empathy, and social cognition Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004. In music, mirror-neuron systems are believed to help us take in and internalise musical gestures, rhythms, and emotions. Watch a musician play or a dancer move and your mirror neurons may fire as though you were doing it yourself. That internal echo supports learning by imitation, but also the embodied, emotional side of music. That is part of why we can “feel” the beat or read a performer’s expression as our own. It also fits the picture from the psychoacoustics chapter: listeners hear sounds as actions on objects, drawing on internalised action–sound couplings, and the motor system appears to take part in that hearing.

That account is popular, and it is also contested:

  • The direct evidence in humans is thin. Mirror neurons were found by recording from single cells in macaques. That is rarely possible in humans, so most human claims rest on indirect measures—fMRI or EEG showing that motor areas are active during observation—which cannot show that the same neurons do both jobs.
  • Activation is not understanding. Critics argue that motor activity during observation may be a consequence of understanding an action rather than its cause; people with damage to motor regions can still recognise actions they can no longer perform Hickok, 2009.
  • The leap to empathy and music is large. Going from “this neuron fires when the monkey grasps and when it sees grasping” to “this is why we feel a performer’s expression” crosses several inferential steps that the data do not license on their own.

This does not mean that the mirror-neuron literature cannot carry over to humans. The coupling between listening and motor systems is robust but the mechanism is still argued over, and that “mirror neurons explain it” is a hypothesis rather than a finding.

Note

Mirror neurons were found by chance in the early 1990s, when a team in Parma recording from the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys noticed cells that fired both when the monkey grasped an object and when it watched someone else do so.

Memory, attention, expectation, learning, and development¶

There are numerous brain processes at work when creating or experiencing music. They run on the regions described above, above all the prefrontal and parietal cortex, the auditory areas and the limbic system, and they change with age, training and culture (see listening on enculturation and expertise).

Memory¶

Memory is usually split into three kinds, the layers that time and rhythm borrowed from Snyder. Sensory memory, called echoic memory for sound, holds the raw sound for a moment after it has stopped. Short-term memory holds a few seconds, about the length of a phrase, and its active part, working memory, holds a short pattern, such as a melodic fragment or a chord change, while we work on it. Long-term memory holds what we have built up over years. Long-term memory has two sides. Explicit memory is what we can recall on purpose: that tune, those lyrics. Implicit memory is what we know without having tried to learn it, such as noticing a “wrong” note in a familiar style.

The hippocampus and the cortex around it recognise familiar music and attach it to our own past. That is why a few seconds of a song can work like a time machine. At the same time, the music we grew up with shapes what we expect of melody and harmony, without our ever learning the rules. This kind of learning does not even need attention. In one study, patients read a book while random tone sequences played in the background. Recordings from inside their brains showed that the structure of the sequences was being learned anyway, in a network far beyond the auditory cortex Fuhrer et al., 2025.

Fun fact

Earworms, the tunes that loop in the head uninvited, visit nearly everyone, and most people at least once a week Liikkanen, 2012. The tunes that stick tend to be faster than average, with a simple, singable contour and a few unexpected leaps, and they are usually songs heard recently or often Jakubowski et al., 2017.

Attention¶

Attention decides which sounds matter right now. Selective attention lets you follow one instrument or voice in a mixture, which explains the cocktail-party problem from psychoacoustics. Divided attention is what you need when several parts must be followed at once. Attention can be chosen (you decide to listen for the bass) or captured (a sudden change in timbre grabs you). It can also be overt, as when your eyes move to a line in the score or to a performer (vision), or covert, when the “listening spotlight” moves while the eyes stay still.

Frontal and parietal networks tell the auditory cortex what to favour. This is why many brain imaging studies manipulate attention on purpose: the same sound gives a different brain response depending on what the listener is attending to.

Expectation¶

Listeners do not only hear what arrives. They hear it against a prediction of what should come next. Exposure builds a model of pitch, timing and style, and a surprise, a break with that model, is a major source of tension, humour and emotion in music (see harmony and melody). The model is built by statistical learning: we pick up the probabilities of how music continues without anyone teaching them.

Dig deeper: event-related potentials and the mismatch negativity

In cognitive neuroscience, event-related potentials (ERPs, explained under EEG below) such as the mismatch negativity (MMN) have been used to track the automatic detection of rule-breaking or rare sounds—even when listeners are not paying attention to the stream—while other components reflect more conscious expectation and updating.

Expectation thus links the sound to music theory. Rhythm gives it a clock. RITMO researchers have found that a sound is perceived better when it arrives at an expected moment, and that both a strict pulse and a learnable irregular pattern create such expectations Solli et al., 2025.

Learning and development¶

Neuroplasticity means that experience changes the connections between neurons. Musical training is linked to differences in auditory, motor and multisensory regions, and to how tightly the motor system follows a heard rhythm. Infants and children tune in early to the music around them. For some abilities, such as parts of pitch learning, there are sensitive periods: windows in which experience leaves an unusually lasting mark. Children generally learn fast, but adults also learn and develop.

Training strengthens the connections between auditory, motor and cognitive regions. Musicians often hear finer differences, remember musical patterns better, and couple hearing and movement more tightly. Age works the other way: the ear and the brain lose some sensitivity, and it becomes harder to pick out speech or fine musical detail, though plasticity allows some compensation.

Over a lifetime, enculturation sets what counts as normal and what counts as surprising. Individual differences come on top. Some people have absolute pitch; people with amusia struggle with pitch or rhythm even when hearing and intelligence are otherwise normal; autistic people may process music differently, sometimes with sharper pitch perception.

Is it nature or culture? Partly nature: how strongly people are moved by music is in part inherited. In a study of about 2,600 Norwegian twins, musical sensibility went together with openness (especially aesthetic sensitivity), agreeableness and negative emotionality, and the links were mostly explained by shared genes Hansen et al., 2025. All of this is why two brains can process the same recording so differently, and why studies that follow people over years, or across cultures, matter as much as laboratory experiments.

Music, emotion, and aesthetic experience¶

Music cognition is not only about analysing pitch and time, it also connects to the brain’s reward, memory and motivation circuits. In musical neuroscience, core affect is the quick shift in pleasantness and arousal that a surprising or familiar sound sets off. Episodic memory is music as a time machine for scenes from one’s own life. Aesthetic judgement is the slower verdict that a piece is beautiful or interesting, which can disagree with how much pleasure it gave. Chills are the clearest case. Physiology described their bodily signature; in the brain, dopamine is released in the striatum at the same moments, in the caudate while the peak is approaching and in the nucleus accumbens when it arrives, so the anticipation and the arrival are rewarded separately Salimpoor et al., 2011. The nostalgia a snippet of melody can set off and a performance that moves us through timing and timbre belong here too. All of them connect the auditory pathways to limbic and prefrontal regions Koelsch, 2014.

Three cautions apply to every result in this area. The same change in the sound can mean different things in different repertoires. A rating on an emotion scale is not the same as what perceivers and critics say about beauty. And a group average says little about any one perceiver, which matters most when a claim is clinical. The physiology chapter’s distinction between perceived and induced emotion applies here too. Many imaging studies also have to separate the sound from the performer’s visible expression, which carries emotion on its own, as the body chapter showed.

Capturing brain activity¶

As for the other signals we have explored in this book, there are also numerous ways of capturing brain activity. A handful of non-invasive techniques are in common use, in both research and the clinic, for watching neural responses during listening and performance. Each one tells us something different about the timing, the location, and the nature of what the brain is doing.

EEG (electroencephalography)¶

Electroencephalography (EEG) records the brain’s electrical activity through electrodes on the scalp. The electrodes pick up the tiny voltages produced when large groups of neurons fire together. The strength of EEG is time. It follows changes from one millisecond to the next, so it is the method for asking when the brain does something. In music research this takes two forms. Event-related potentials (ERPs) are the small responses locked to a note or a chord, and they show attention, expectation and memory at work as the music unfolds. The rhythms of the EEG itself can also lock to a beat.

Research assistants and students are practising EEG measurement at RITMO (Photo: UiO).

The weakness of EEG is the lack of good spatial resolution. The scalp blurs where a signal comes from, and muscle movement and eye blinks leave artefacts. Playing an instrument while wearing an EEG cap is possible but awkward.

At RITMO, we use everything from consumer headbands with four dry electrodes on the forehead to multi-channel gel electrodes on research caps. Four electrodes are enough to see alpha rise when the eyes close, and even some event-related potentials Krigolson et al., 2017, but not to say where in the brain a signal comes from. Muscle tension in the forehead and jaw shows up strongly at those sites. The newest consumer headbands add a few fNIRS light sensors beside the electrodes, so the same band reads both the fast electrical rhythms and the slower blood-oxygen change in the prefrontal cortex; the Muse S Athena, shown in class, has four EEG electrodes and five such light sensors along the brow.

EEG is usually summarised by the power in standard frequency bands, delta, theta, alpha, and beta, from the slowest waves to the fastest. Here we synthesise a signal dominated by alpha waves, estimate its power spectrum with Welch’s method (a standard averaging technique), and read off the band powers. That is the first step of most real analyses.

EEG is also used to study the response to single events, such as a chord or a note. Each response is tiny next to the ongoing activity, so researchers present the same event many times and average the recordings. Then one can see when the ongoing activity cancels out, and the time-locked response, an event-related potential (ERP), emerges. The simulation below shows this happening with synthetic data.

MEG (magnetoencephalography)¶

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures the faint magnetic fields produced by the same neural currents. It keeps the millisecond timing of EEG and locates the sources better, because the skull distorts magnetic fields less than it distorts voltages. MEG is therefore the method for asking when and roughly where at once, and for watching how regions synchronise during a musical task. However, the machine is big and expensive and requires a magnetically shielded room, and the participant has to sit still, so MEG studies of music are studies of listening and imagining, not of playing.

MEG instrument used for recording magnetic fields generated by brain activity (Photo: Very Big Brain).

fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging)¶

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) does not measure neural activity itself. An active brain region uses more oxygen, which changes the local blood, and the scanner maps that change through the whole brain at millimetre scale. fMRI has poor temporal resolution but high spatial, which means that it excels at answering questions about where in the brain something happens. It is what showed that listening engages not only the auditory cortex but also limbic regions, the prefrontal cortex and motor areas. Its limits follow from what it measures. The blood response takes seconds, so fMRI cannot resolve the timing of individual notes. The scanner is also loud and confined, and the participant must lie still, so music is heard through noise-cancelling headphones. It is also expensive, and off-limits to anyone with metal implants.

Example of an fMRI scan (Image: Wikimedia Commons).

fNIRS (functional near-infrared spectroscopy)¶

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) measures the same blood-oxygen change as fMRI, but with light. A cap of small sources shines near-infrared light into the scalp, and detectors next to them read what comes back. fNIRS sees only the outer layer of the cortex, and more coarsely than fMRI. But it is silent, portable and handles motion quite well. That means it is the most versatile capture method in real-world musical settings.

Victoria Johnson performing with an fNIRS system during the event MusicLab Brain: Inside the mind of a violinist in 2024 (Photo: UiO).

Comparison of brain activity measurement methods¶

Together, EEG, MEG, fMRI and fNIRS cover the whole range, from the millisecond timing of neural events to the location of the regions behind perception, cognition and emotion.

MethodMeasuresBest atSampling
EEGElectrical activity at the scalpWhen (ms)250–1000 Hz
MEGMagnetic fields from the same currentsWhen, and roughly where~1000 Hz
fMRIBlood oxygen, whole brainWhere (mm)One image per 1–2 s
fNIRSBlood oxygen, outer cortexWhere, while moving~10 Hz

Each method has its own trade-off:

  • EEG is cheap and portable and works with almost anyone, but it is blurred in space, sees mainly the surface, and picks up muscle and blink artefacts.
  • MEG, like EEG, measures neural activity directly and locates its sources better, but needs a shielded room, costs a great deal, and tolerates no movement.
  • fMRI gives millimetre maps of the whole brain, but is slow, loud, confined and costly, and unsuitable for some people.
  • fNIRS is silent, portable and tolerant of movement, but sees only the outer cortex, and coarsely.

The sampling column follows the same logic as the signal table in physiology: the rate must comfortably exceed twice the fastest change of interest. Neural oscillations reach into the gamma band, roughly 30–100 Hz, so EEG and MEG sample at hundreds to thousands of hertz. fMRI does not sample electrical activity at all. It samples a blood-oxygen response that takes several seconds, so one image every second or two is enough, and a faster scanner would change nothing.

Clinical and creative applications¶

The methods above were primarily developed for analytic purposes, but they have also been explored for control.

Brain–computer interfaces¶

A brain–computer interface (BCI) lets the brain control a device directly, without going through muscles or speech. For sound and music this opens three kinds of use.

  • Making music: EEG-based systems let people compose melodies, trigger sounds, or change a sound’s parameters by imagining a movement or focusing their attention. fNIRS is a newer control signal for the same purpose, slower than EEG but less disturbed by muscle activity, and now cheap enough to sit in a consumer headband Naseer & Hong, 2015. For people with physical disabilities this is a new route to musical expression.

  • Hearing aids: Modern hearing aids already adapt to the surroundings. Researchers are asking whether a hearing aid could also read which sound the user is attending to, and bring that voice forward in a noisy room. In the laboratory, the voice a listener attends to can already be decoded from EEG O'Sullivan et al., 2015, but not yet quickly or reliably enough for a device worn every day.

  • Cochlear implants: A cochlear implant restores hearing by stimulating the auditory nerve directly. Researchers are exploring whether an implant could adjust its settings from the user’s own brain activity.

The common idea is that reading brain activity can sharpen hearing, communication and creative expression. Outside music-making, most of this is still at the research stage.

Music therapy¶

The clinical thread continues into therapy. Music therapy is the planned use of music by a trained therapist to reach a health goal, such as regaining movement, speech or social contact Wheeler, 2015. Since music engages motor, language, memory and reward networks at once, it is also an excellent tool for exercise and development. Some examples:

  • Walking in Parkinson’s disease: People with Parkinson’s disease often walk with short, irregular steps. Walking to a steady musical beat can make the stride longer and steadier Ghai et al., 2018. It works through the coupling between hearing and movement described earlier in this chapter.
  • Speaking again after a stroke: Melodic intonation therapy helps people with aphasia, the loss of language after brain damage. They sing short phrases before speaking them, which may recruit song networks that survive when the usual speech regions are damaged. The trials are small and their results mixed Zumbansen et al., 2014.
  • Memory in dementia: Familiar songs often remain reachable in dementia long after names and recent events have gone. Music-based interventions probably reduce depressive symptoms, but may make little difference to agitation Steen et al., 2018. Singing and personal playlists can also open moments of contact with family and carers.

It is important to separate music therapy as a research field and profession from music as self-care. A professional music therapist assesses a client, sets goals, chooses methods and documents the outcome, within a health service and its rules. In Norway, music therapy is an established profession, with programmes at the Norwegian Academy of Music in Oslo and the University of Bergen.

A critical look: do music lessons make children smarter?¶

The Mozart effect from the first chapter has a longer-lived relative. Music schools, instrument makers, and well-meaning parents repeat that learning an instrument raises intelligence and school grades, and the claim is used to argue for music in the curriculum. But is it true?

  • The claim is that music training transfers to abilities far from music: IQ, memory, mathematics, reading. Psychologists call a gain in something unrelated to what was trained far transfer.
  • The evidence began well. In a randomised study, six-year-olds who had a year of keyboard or singing lessons gained about three IQ points more than children who had drama lessons or none Schellenberg, 2004. The effect was small. The picture changed when the whole literature was pooled. A meta-analysis of 54 studies with almost seven thousand children found a small average benefit. It shrank to nothing once the quality of the study design was taken into account. The best-designed studies, with random assignment and an active control activity, found no effect on cognitive or academic outcomes at all Sala & Gobet, 2020.
  • The method explains the gap. Most positive results are correlational. Children who take music lessons also tend to have better-off, more educated and more involved parents, and they differ in personality. The lessons cannot be separated from everything that comes with them. Experiments that assign children at random, and give the control group something equally engaging, remove that confound, and the effect goes with it.
  • The limits cut the other way too. That there is no far transfer does not mean music training does nothing. Near transfer, a gain in something close to what was trained, is well documented: music lessons sharpen hearing skills such as telling two pitches apart, they make children better musicians, and the structural brain differences in musicians described earlier are genuine consequences of practice.

The lesson mirrors the Mozart effect: the brain is plastic, practice changes it, and a change in the brain is not the same as a general gain in intelligence. Music lessons make children better at music, and that is reason enough to give them. There is also a well-being case that does not depend on intelligence: in a review of over 900 studies for the World Health Organization, singing, playing and listening were associated with lower stress and anxiety, better mood and more social contact Fancourt & Finn, 2019. Much of that evidence is correlational too, so the same questions about method apply, but the outcomes it measures are the ones music is actually for.

Chapter summary

This chapter followed sound from the cochlea to the cortex and beyond. The auditory pathway reads out pitch, loudness and timbre; the prefrontal cortex adds attention and expectation, the motor cortex the beat, and the limbic and reward systems feeling and memory, with chills as the moment where the two meet. Memory comes in layers, attention chooses, expectation predicts, and training, culture and in part inheritance decide why two brains hear the same recording differently. Mirror neurons are a popular but contested account of why watching a performer feels like doing it. Four methods watch the brain at work: EEG for when, fMRI for where, MEG for both at a price, and fNIRS for people who move; the newest consumer headbands combine the first and the last. Brain–computer interfaces and music therapy put the same knowledge to use. The critical look showed that music lessons change the brain and make children better at music, but the best studies find no general gain in intelligence; the case for music and well-being rests on other outcomes.

Questions
  1. Trace a sung note from the cochlea to the auditory cortex, and name one thing each that the frontal, motor and limbic regions add to what you hear.
  2. How do sensory, short-term and long-term memory each take part in following a song, and what does an earworm tell you about musical memory?
  3. Mirror neurons are offered as the reason that watching a performer feels like doing it. State the claim, one piece of evidence for it, and one caveat.
  4. A researcher wants to study a violinist’s brain while they play with an orchestra. Which of EEG, MEG, fMRI and fNIRS would you choose, and what do you give up?
  5. The best-designed studies find no intelligence gain from music lessons. What do they find instead, and what would you say to a parent who asks whether the lessons are worth it?
