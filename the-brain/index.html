11. The brain

How the brain processes sound and music

Everything we have studied so far ends up here. This is the interpretation level of the four levels of description, and also the place where the ladder folds back on itself: expectation and attention change what the auditory cortex does to a signal, so the highest level reaches down and alters the lowest. The cochlea and psychoacoustics, embodiment, and physiological reactions all feed a brain that turns vibrating air into pitch, rhythm, timbre, emotion, and memory. The signal is reshaped at every step as it travels from the ear to the cortex and the limbic system. Harmony and melody described pitch and tonal structure in musical terms; neuroscience often takes those same dimensions into the laboratory and manipulates them. This week looks at which brain regions are doing the work, how they cooperate, and how researchers measure their activity while we listen and perform.

The human brain¶

Some 86 billion neurons, plus an even larger number of supporting glial cells, make the brain the most intricate organ we have. It is where all sensory input is gathered and where perception, cognition, emotion, and movement are coordinated. The brain is also remarkably plastic. It reorganises itself in response to musical training, sensory experience, and even injury. That is why we can pick up new musical skills, adapt after hearing loss, and grow particular musical tastes. By weaving together signals from the outside world and from our own internal states, it lets us interpret sound and music and find meaning in them. Its main anatomical regions include:

  • Frontal lobe: At the front of the brain, this region handles executive functions—decision-making, planning, problem-solving, voluntary movement—and shapes aspects of personality and social behaviour.

  • Parietal lobe: Sitting behind the frontal lobe, it processes bodily sensation: touch, temperature, pain, and spatial orientation.

  • Temporal lobe: Tucked beneath the frontal and parietal lobes, it carries much of our auditory processing, language comprehension, and memory formation.

  • Occipital lobe: At the back of the brain, this region is devoted mainly to vision.

  • Cerebellum: Below the occipital lobe, it coordinates voluntary movement, balance, posture, and motor learning.

  • Cingulate gyrus: A part of the limbic system, it contributes to emotion, learning, and memory, and ties behavioural outcomes to motivation.

Illustration of the brain’s lobes (Wikimedia Commons).

Note

The terms brain and mind are often used interchangeably, but they refer to different things. The brain is a physical organ made up of billions of neurons and supporting cells. The mind is the set of mental processes and experiences that arise from brain activity: thoughts, emotions, memories, consciousness, and self-awareness. The mind is not a physical object but the result of complex interactions within the brain and between the brain and its environment.

Neurons¶

Neurons are the cells that carry signals in the brain and the rest of the nervous system. They do so with electricity inside the cell and with chemistry between cells. A neuron has three main parts. The cell body, or soma, holds the nucleus. The dendrites are branches that receive signals from other neurons. The axon is a long fibre that carries the neuron’s own signal away, to other neurons, to muscles, or to glands.

Illustration of a multipolar neuron (Wikimedia Commons).

Neurons do not touch. Where an axon meets the next dendrite there is a tiny gap, the synapse. The signal crosses it as a chemical: the axon releases neurotransmitters, and the dendrite on the other side picks them up. A neuron adds up everything its dendrites receive. If the sum is large enough, it fires an action potential, a brief electrical pulse that runs down the axon to the next synapse. Billions of cells doing this, many times a second, is what hears a melody, moves a hand, and remembers a song.

Dig deeper: artificial neural networks

The brain has also served as a design metaphor. Artificial neural networks (ANNs), the machinery behind most of what is now called artificial intelligence, take their name and their basic picture from the cells described above: many simple units, each summing its inputs and passing on a result, wired together in layers whose connection strengths change with use.

The analogy is worth holding loosely. A network’s “learning” is the adjustment of numerical weights to reduce an error, which is a thin cartoon of synaptic plasticity, and its units have none of the chemistry, timing, or anatomy of a neuron. The traffic between the two fields has nonetheless run both ways. Neuroscience lent machine learning a metaphor, and trained networks are now used in return as explicit hypotheses about what the auditory system might be computing, to be tested against recordings from real brains. Machine listening takes the topic up on the technical side.

The listening brain¶

Hearing begins in the ear, but listening is done by the brain. The regions and pathways below are the ones that turn the signal from the cochlea into a heard sound, and then into a melody, a beat, a memory, and a feeling.

Key brain regions for listening¶

A handful of regions do much of the work when we process sound and music:

  • The auditory cortex, in the temporal lobe, decodes basic sound features such as pitch, loudness, and timbre, and is central to recognising and interpreting musical elements.
  • The prefrontal cortex handles higher-order work: directing attention, spotting patterns, and predicting where the music is going.
  • The motor cortex lights up even when we only listen, reflecting how the brain responds to rhythm and beat and prepares for movement Zatorre et al., 2007.
  • The limbic system, which includes the amygdala and hippocampus, drives emotional responses to music and ties pieces to memories.
  • The nucleus accumbens, part of the reward system, is linked to the pleasure and motivation that music can stir up Salimpoor et al., 2011.

Auditory pathways¶

Psychoacoustics followed sound as far as the cochlea, where hair cells turn vibration into nerve impulses, ordered by frequency. This chapter picks the signal up from there. The auditory nerve carries it to the brainstem. The first stop is the cochlear nuclei. From there the signal climbs through a chain of relay stations in the brainstem, the midbrain and the thalamus before it reaches the cortex.

Dig deeper: the relay stations of the auditory pathway
  • The superior olivary complex, in the brainstem, compares the timing and intensity of sound at the two ears, which is the basis for locating sounds in space.
  • The inferior colliculus, in the midbrain, gathers auditory information and supports reflexive responses such as turning the head towards a noise.
  • The medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus works as a relay hub, routing auditory signals on to the right parts of the cerebral cortex.

The last stop is the primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. Here the basic features are read out: pitch, loudness and timbre. Neighbouring areas then take over and recognise larger things, such as a voice, a word, or a tune. Patel (2007) is the standard account of how much of this machinery music shares with language.

Two things make the pathway powerful. It is a hierarchy, so each stage builds on the one below. It is also parallel, so several features of a sound are analysed at the same time. That is why we can hear where a sound comes from, what made it, and what it means, all at once.

Neural processing of sound¶

In the auditory cortex, different groups of neurons respond to different features of the sound:

  • Pitch: Neurons are laid out by the frequency they respond to, low at one end and high at the other, a tonotopic map like the one in the cochlea.
  • Loudness: A louder sound makes the neurons fire faster.
  • Timbre: The pattern across many frequencies at once tells one instrument or voice from another.
  • Rhythm and timing: Patterns in time are tracked by the auditory cortex together with motor areas, which is why we can find and follow a beat.
Dig deeper: lateralisation

Lateralisation describes how some auditory functions lean more on one hemisphere than the other. The left hemisphere tends to specialise in rapid temporal changes, like those in speech and rhythm, which makes it important for language comprehension and rhythmic analysis. The right hemisphere is generally more sensitive to spectral, frequency-based information, and so matters more for melody, pitch, and the emotional shading of music. Splitting the work this way lets the brain analyse complex sounds like music by handling different features in parallel.

The features are then combined with what other regions contribute. The prefrontal cortex adds attention and expectation. The limbic system adds feeling. The motor cortex adds the urge to move. This is where a sound becomes a familiar tune, where we sense where it is going, and where it links to a memory.

Music cognition¶

Music cognition is the mental work of making sense of music. Listening goes far beyond analysing sound: we recognise a melody, expect the next chord, feel the rhythm, and remember where we heard it. Several regions share this work:

  • The prefrontal cortex finds patterns, builds expectations, and holds a phrase in working memory.
  • The motor cortex tracks the beat and plans movement, even when we sit still.
  • The limbic system, with the amygdala and hippocampus, links the music to emotion and to memories from our own life.
  • The reward system, especially the nucleus accumbens, responds at the moments we find pleasurable.

Together these networks let us follow the structure, guess what comes next, feel something, and tie the music to our past. How they cooperate in detail is still an open research question.

Memory, attention, expectation, learning, and development¶

Perception is only the way in. While music plays, memory, attention, expectation and learning are all at work. They run on the regions described above, above all the prefrontal and parietal cortex, the auditory areas and the limbic system, and they change with age, training and culture (see listening on enculturation and expertise).

Memory¶

Memory is usually split into three kinds. Sensory memory holds a sound for a second or so after it has stopped. Working memory holds a short pattern, such as a melodic fragment or a chord change, while we work on it. Long-term memory holds what we have built up over years. Long-term memory has two sides. Explicit memory is what we can recall on purpose: that tune, those lyrics. Implicit memory is what we know without having tried to learn it, such as noticing a “wrong” note in a familiar style.

The hippocampus and the cortex around it recognise familiar music and attach it to our own past. That is why a few seconds of a song can work like a time machine. At the same time, the music we grew up with shapes what we expect of melody and harmony, without our ever learning the rules. This kind of learning does not even need attention. In one study, patients read a book while random tone sequences played in the background. Recordings from inside their brains showed that the structure of the sequences was being learned anyway, in a network far beyond the auditory cortex Fuhrer et al., 2025. Earworms, the tunes that loop in the head uninvited, show that musical memory can also start itself.

Attention¶

Attention decides which sounds matter right now. Selective attention lets you follow one instrument or voice in a mixture, the cocktail-party problem from psychoacoustics. Divided attention is what you need when several parts must be followed at once. Attention can be chosen (you decide to listen for the bass) or captured (a sudden change in timbre grabs you). It can also be overt, as when your eyes move to a line in the score or to a performer (vision), or covert, when the “listening spotlight” moves while the eyes stay still.

Frontal and parietal networks tell the auditory cortex what to favour. This is why many EEG and fMRI studies manipulate attention on purpose: the same sound gives a different brain response depending on what the listener is attending to.

Expectation¶

Listeners do not only hear what arrives. They hear it against a prediction of what should come next. Exposure builds a model of pitch, timing and style, and a surprise, a break with that model, is a major source of tension, humour and emotion in music (see harmony and melody). The model is built by statistical learning: we pick up the probabilities of how music continues without anyone teaching them.

Dig deeper: event-related potentials and the mismatch negativity

In cognitive neuroscience, event-related potentials (ERPs) such as the mismatch negativity (MMN) have been used to track the automatic detection of rule-breaking or rare sounds—even when listeners are not paying attention to the stream—while other components reflect more conscious expectation and updating.

Expectation thus links the sound to music theory. Rhythm gives it a clock. EEG work at the University of Oslo shows that a sound is perceived better when it arrives at an expected moment, and that both a strict pulse and a learnable irregular pattern create such expectations Solli et al., 2025.

Learning and development¶

Neuroplasticity means that experience changes the connections between neurons. Musical training is linked to differences in auditory, motor and multisensory regions, and to how tightly the motor system follows a heard rhythm. Development adds time. Infants and children tune in early to the music around them. For some abilities, such as parts of pitch learning, there are sensitive periods: windows in which experience leaves an unusually lasting mark. That does not mean adults learn nothing.

Over a lifetime, enculturation sets what counts as normal and what counts as surprising. Training, personality and neurodiversity add individual differences on top. All of this is why two brains can process the same recording so differently, and why studies that follow people over years, or across cultures, matter as much as laboratory experiments.

Music, emotion, and aesthetic experience¶

Music reaches the brain’s reward, memory and motivation circuits, not only the circuits that analyse pitch and time. Neuroscientists separate three things that everyday language runs together. Core affect is the quick shift in pleasantness and arousal that a surprising or familiar sound sets off. Episodic memory is music as a time machine for scenes from one’s own life. Aesthetic judgement is the slower verdict that a piece is beautiful or interesting, which can disagree with how much pleasure it gave. Chills, the nostalgia a snippet of melody can set off, and a performance that moves us through timing and timbre all belong here. All of them connect the auditory pathways to limbic and prefrontal regions Koelsch, 2014.

Three cautions apply to every result in this area. The same change in the sound can mean different things in different repertoires. A rating on an emotion scale is not the same as what perceivers and critics say about beauty. And a group average says little about any one perceiver, which matters most when a claim is clinical. The physiology chapter’s distinction between perceived and induced emotion applies here too. Many imaging studies also have to separate the sound from the performer’s visible expression, which carries emotion on its own, as the body chapter showed.

Mirror neurons¶

Mirror neurons are a special class of cell that fire both when you perform an action and when you watch someone else perform the same one. First found in the premotor cortex of monkeys, they have since been identified in humans and are thought to matter for understanding actions, imitation, empathy, and social cognition Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004. In music, mirror-neuron systems are believed to help us take in and internalise musical gestures, rhythms, and emotions. Watch a musician play or a dancer move and your mirror neurons may fire as though you were doing it yourself. That internal echo supports learning by imitation, but also the embodied, emotional side of music. That is part of why we can “feel” the beat or read a performer’s expression as our own. It also fits the picture from the psychoacoustics chapter: listeners hear sounds as actions on objects, drawing on internalised action–sound couplings, and the motor system appears to take part in that hearing.

That account is popular, and it is also contested. Three caveats are worth carrying with you:

  • The direct evidence in humans is thin. Mirror neurons were found by recording from single cells in macaques. That is rarely possible in humans, so most human claims rest on indirect measures—fMRI or EEG showing that motor areas are active during observation—which cannot show that the same neurons do both jobs.
  • Activation is not understanding. Critics, Gregory Hickok most prominently Hickok, 2009, argue that motor activity during observation may be a consequence of understanding an action rather than its cause; people with damage to motor regions can still recognise actions they can no longer perform.
  • The leap to empathy and music is large. Going from “this neuron fires when the monkey grasps and when it sees grasping” to “this is why we feel a performer’s expression” crosses several inferential steps that the data do not license on their own.

None of this means the mirror-neuron literature is worthless. The coupling between listening and motor systems is robust and shows up throughout this course. It means the mechanism is still argued over, and that “mirror neurons explain it” is a hypothesis rather than a finding.

Note

Mirror neurons were first identified in the early 1990s by a team of Italian researchers studying the brains of macaque monkeys. The discovery was serendipitous. While recording neural activity in the premotor cortex, the scientists noticed that certain neurons fired not only when the monkey performed a specific action (such as grasping an object), but also when the monkey observed a human or another monkey performing the same action. This unexpected finding revealed a new class of neurons that “mirrored” observed behaviours, leading to a surge of research into their role in action understanding, imitation, and social cognition. The accidental nature of this discovery highlights how chance observations can open up entirely new fields of neuroscience.

Individual differences¶

People differ widely in how they hear and process music. Genes, development, experience and culture all play a part. Some people are more sensitive to pitch, rhythm or timbre than others. Some have unusual abilities, such as absolute pitch, or unusually strong emotional reactions. How strongly people are moved by music is itself partly inherited. In a study of about 2,600 Norwegian twins, musical sensibility went together with openness (especially aesthetic sensitivity), agreeableness and negative emotionality, and the links were mostly explained by shared genes Hansen et al., 2025.

Training counts for a lot. It strengthens the connections between auditory, motor and cognitive regions. Musicians often hear finer differences, remember musical patterns better, and couple hearing and movement more tightly. Their training can also strengthen the links between the two hemispheres, which demanding skills such as sight-reading and improvising rely on.

Age matters too. With age, the ear and the brain lose some sensitivity, and it becomes harder to pick out speech or fine musical detail. Plasticity allows some compensation, and staying musically active helps keep hearing and thinking in shape.

Neurodiversity adds further variation. People with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), for instance, may process music differently, sometimes with sharper pitch perception or distinctive emotional responses. At the other end, amusia (tone deafness) can limit the perception of pitch or rhythm even when hearing and intelligence are otherwise normal.

Culture leaves its mark as well. Early experience with the music, language and rhythm of one culture shapes neural pathways and tastes, and colours how unfamiliar styles sound later.

What runs through all of this is the brain’s adaptability. Through training, experience and adjustment to sensory loss, it keeps reorganising itself. That is what lets us learn new musical skills, adapt after hearing loss, and develop our own preferences.

Capturing brain activity¶

To study how the brain handles sound and music, researchers need ways to measure its activity. A handful of non-invasive techniques are in common use, in both research and the clinic, for watching neural responses during listening and performance. Each one tells us something different about the timing, the location, and the nature of what the brain is doing.

EEG (electroencephalography)¶

Electroencephalography (EEG) records the brain’s electrical activity through electrodes on the scalp. The electrodes pick up the tiny voltages produced when large groups of neurons fire together. The strength of EEG is time. It follows changes from one millisecond to the next, so it is the method for asking when the brain does something. In music research this takes two forms. Event-related potentials (ERPs) are the small responses locked to a note or a chord, and they show attention, expectation and memory at work as the music unfolds. The rhythms of the EEG itself can also lock to a beat. The weakness of EEG is place. The scalp blurs where a signal comes from, and muscle movement and eye blinks leave artefacts. Playing an instrument in an EEG cap is possible but awkward. EEG is cheap and portable, which is why it is the method of the lab session in this course.

Research assistants and students are practising EEG measurement at RITMO (Photo: UiO).

EEG is usually summarised by the power in standard frequency bands, named delta, theta, alpha, and beta, running from the slowest waves to the fastest. Here we synthesise a signal dominated by alpha waves, estimate its power spectrum with Welch’s method (a standard averaging technique), and read off the band powers, which is the same first step used in real analyses.

EEG is also used to study the response to single events, such as a chord or a note. Each response is tiny next to the ongoing activity, so researchers present the same event many times and average the recordings: the ongoing activity cancels out, and the time-locked response, an event-related potential (ERP), emerges. MEG (magnetoencephalography)¶

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures the faint magnetic fields produced by the same neural currents. It keeps the millisecond timing of EEG and locates the sources better, because the skull distorts magnetic fields less than it distorts voltages. MEG is therefore the method for asking when and roughly where at once, and for watching how regions synchronise during a musical task. The price is the machine: a sensor helmet in a magnetically shielded room, expensive and rare. The participant has to sit still, so MEG studies of music are studies of listening and imagining, not of playing.

MEG instrument used for recording magnetic fields generated by brain activity (Photo: Very Big Brain).

fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging)¶

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) does not measure neural activity itself but one of its consequences. An active region uses more oxygen, which changes the local blood, and the scanner maps that change through the whole brain at millimetre scale. fMRI is the method for asking where. It is what showed that listening engages not only the auditory cortex but also limbic regions, the prefrontal cortex and motor areas. Its limits follow from what it measures. The blood response takes seconds, so fMRI cannot resolve the timing of individual notes. The scanner is loud and confined, and the participant must lie still, so the music arrives through headphones over the noise and nobody plays. It is also expensive, and off-limits to anyone with metal implants.

Example of an fMRI scan (Image: Wikimedia Commons).

fNIRS (functional near-infrared spectroscopy)¶

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) measures the same blood-oxygen change as fMRI, but with light. A cap of small sources shines near-infrared light through the scalp, and detectors next to them read what comes back. fNIRS sees only the outer layer of the cortex, and more coarsely than fMRI. In return it is silent, portable and tolerant of movement. That takes it to the situations the other methods cannot reach: infants and children, musicians playing their instruments, and groups making music together.

Victoria Johnson performing with an fNIRS system during the event MusicLab Brain: Inside the mind of a violinist in 2024 (Photo: UiO).

Comparison of brain activity measurement methods¶

Together, EEG, MEG, fMRI and fNIRS cover the whole range, from the millisecond timing of neural events to the location of the regions behind perception, cognition and emotion. Each makes its own compromise, so the method follows from the question.

MethodMeasuresBest atSampling
EEGElectrical activity at the scalpWhen (ms)250–1000 Hz
MEGMagnetic fields from the same currentsWhen, and roughly where~1000 Hz
fMRIBlood oxygen, whole brainWhere (mm)One image per 1–2 s
fNIRSBlood oxygen, outer cortexWhere, while moving~10 Hz

Each method has its own trade-off:

  • EEG is cheap and portable and works with almost anyone, but it is blurred in space, sees mainly the surface, and picks up muscle and blink artefacts.
  • MEG locates sources better and measures neural activity directly, but needs a shielded room, costs a great deal, and tolerates no movement.
  • fMRI gives millimetre maps of the whole brain, but is slow, loud, confined and costly, and unsuitable for some people.
  • fNIRS is silent, portable and tolerant of movement, but sees only the outer cortex, and coarsely.

The sampling column follows the same logic as the physiology table in the previous chapter: the rate must comfortably exceed twice the fastest change of interest. Neural oscillations reach into the gamma band, roughly 30–100 Hz, so EEG and MEG sample at hundreds to thousands of hertz. fMRI does not sample electrical activity at all but a blood-oxygen response that takes several seconds, so one image every second or two is enough, and no faster scanner would change that.

Clinical and creative applications¶

The methods above were developed to observe the brain. Two fields use them to act on it or with it. Brain-computer interfaces turn brain activity into control signals. Music therapy uses music to change what the brain does.

Brain-computer interfaces¶

A brain-computer interface (BCI) lets the brain control a device directly, without going through muscles or speech. For sound and music this opens three kinds of use.

  • Making music: EEG-based systems let people compose melodies, trigger sounds, or change a sound’s parameters by imagining a movement or focusing their attention. For people with physical disabilities this is a new route to musical expression.

  • Hearing aids: Modern hearing aids already adapt to the surroundings. Researchers are asking whether a hearing aid could also read which sound the user is attending to, and bring that voice forward in a noisy room.

  • Cochlear implants: A cochlear implant restores hearing by stimulating the auditory nerve directly. Future implants may respond to the user’s own brain activity, which could give better sound quality and clearer speech.

The common idea is that reading brain activity can sharpen hearing, communication and creative expression. As the technology matures, such devices will matter more in both clinical and artistic work.

Music therapy¶

The clinical thread continues into therapy. Music therapy is the planned use of music by a trained therapist to reach a health goal, such as regaining movement, speech or social contact Wheeler, 2015. Music engages motor, language, memory and reward networks at once. That gives it routes into the brain that words or exercise alone sometimes lack. Three uses have particularly solid evidence:

  • Walking in Parkinson’s disease: People with Parkinson’s disease often walk with short, shuffling, irregular steps. Walking to a steady musical beat, called rhythmic auditory stimulation, can make the stride longer and steadier. It works through the coupling between hearing and movement described earlier in this chapter.
  • Speaking again after a stroke: Melodic intonation therapy helps people with aphasia, the loss of language after brain damage. They sing short phrases before speaking them, which recruits song networks that can survive when the usual speech regions are damaged.
  • Memory in dementia: Familiar songs often remain reachable in dementia long after names and recent events have gone. Singing and personal playlists can reduce agitation and open moments of contact with family and carers.

It helps to separate music therapy as a profession from music as self-care. A music therapist assesses a client, sets goals, chooses methods and documents the outcome, within a health service and its rules. A running playlist or a wind-down album can be good for you, but it is not therapy in this sense. In Norway, music therapy is an established profession, with programmes at the Norwegian Academy of Music in Oslo and the University of Bergen.

Music and wellbeing

Students heading towards psychology may want to read further on music and health. The collection edited by MacDonald et al. (2012) maps the research field, from clinical music therapy to everyday listening. A critical look: do music lessons make children smarter?¶

The Mozart effect from the first chapter has a longer-lived relative. Music schools, instrument makers, and well-meaning parents repeat that learning an instrument raises intelligence and school grades, and the claim is used to argue for music in the curriculum. It deserves a careful reading, because the brain changes described in this chapter are real and are easily stretched into promises.

  • The claim is that music training transfers to abilities far from music: IQ, memory, mathematics, reading.
  • The evidence began well. In a randomised study, six-year-olds who had a year of keyboard or singing lessons gained about three IQ points more than children who had drama lessons or none Schellenberg, 2004. The effect was small, but it was reported as large. The picture changed when the whole literature was pooled. A meta-analysis of 54 studies with almost seven thousand children found a small average benefit. It shrank to nothing once the quality of the study design was taken into account. The best-designed studies, with random assignment and an active control activity, found no effect on cognitive or academic outcomes at all Sala & Gobet, 2020.
  • The method explains the gap. Most positive results are correlational. Children who take music lessons also tend to have better-off, more educated and more involved parents, and they differ in personality. The lessons cannot be separated from everything that comes with them. Experiments that assign children at random, and give the control group something equally engaging, remove that confound, and the effect goes with it.
  • The limits cut the other way too. No far transfer does not mean music training does nothing. Near transfer, to auditory skills such as pitch discrimination and to musical ability itself, is well documented, and the structural brain differences in musicians described earlier are genuine consequences of practice.

The lesson mirrors the Mozart effect: the brain is plastic, practice changes it, and a change in the brain is not the same as a general gain in intelligence. Music lessons make children better at music, and that is reason enough to give them.

Popular-science coverage of music and the brain is a good hunting ground. Claims there often outrun the method used to support them.

Chapter summary

This chapter followed sound from the cochlea to the cortex and beyond. The auditory pathway reads out pitch, loudness and timbre; the prefrontal cortex adds attention and expectation, the motor cortex the beat, and the limbic and reward systems feeling and memory. Memory, attention, expectation and learning shape every listening, and training and culture rewire the brain over a lifetime. Mirror neurons are a popular but contested account of why watching a performer feels like doing it. Four methods watch the brain at work: EEG for when, fMRI for where, MEG for both at a price, and fNIRS for people who move. Brain-computer interfaces and music therapy put the same knowledge to use. The critical look showed that music lessons change the brain and make children better at music, but the best studies find no general gain in intelligence.

  2. How do working memory, long-term knowledge, and implicit statistical learning each contribute to hearing structure and surprise in music?

  4. What trade-offs separate EEG, MEG, fMRI, and fNIRS when studying musical listening or performance?
