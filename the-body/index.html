8. The body

The body in music performance, perception, and analysis

So far, we have treated sound and music as sound waves in the air, audio recordings, or auditory stimuli. But we also perceive music through our bodies. This week we will investigate a performer’s gestures as physical events, perceptual cues, and interpretive acts at the same time. The core argument of embodied music cognition is that the body shapes how we experience music. We will also consider some anatomy and biomechanics and methods for analysing human movement.

Question

How do you think about your own body in relation to music performance or perception?

Embodied music cognition¶

We discussed embodied music cognition briefly at the start of the course. An embodied approach stresses the role of the body in producing, perceiving, and understanding music. Rather than treating musical experience as sound alone, it shows how bodily sensations, movements, and actions carry musical meaning.

Three ideas recur through the chapter: perception and action form a loop; musical experience draws on several senses at once; and gesture carries meaning.

Dig deeper: the three ideas spelled out

  • Sensorimotor coupling: listening to music often sets the body going, tapping a foot, nodding, swaying, which shows how close perception and action are. This is the action–perception loop: perceiving music can prompt movement, and movement in turn shapes how the music is experienced.

  • Multimodal integration: musical experience is multimodal. It brings together sound, sight, and kinaesthetic information (movement and bodily sensation), and the body coordinates the streams. Watching a performer’s gestures can sharpen our sense of phrasing; feeling vibration or movement can deepen our sense of rhythm.

  • Gesture and imagery: expressive body movements, from large gestures to small sound-facilitating actions, help musicians communicate, phrase, and convey emotion. They shape the sound, and they shape how performers and perceivers represent music in their minds. Even without moving, musicians and perceivers often use motor imagery, simulating the actions tied to a piece, which supports learning, memory, and interpretation.

The Belgian systematic musicologist Marc Leman popularised the term in the early 2000s. In Embodied Music Cognition and Mediation Technology Leman, 2007 he traces the processes at work in the bodies of performers and perceivers, and describes how musical intention rests on sonic and visual communication between them:

4E cognition¶

In recent years, the idea of 4E cognition has gained ground, building on the “embodied turn” in cognitive science and philosophy of mind. The 4E framework—standing for Embodied, Embedded, Enactive, and Extended cognition—challenges the traditional view that cognition is confined to the brain.

Researchers such as Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, Alva Noë, and Andy Clark argue that understanding cognition means taking the body, the environment, and their interactions into account. The four cognitive processes included in 4E are:

  • Embodied: Our perception and understanding of music are rooted in the body. How we move, breathe, and physically interact with instruments or our surroundings shapes how we experience and interpret sound. Tapping a foot to a beat or feeling vibrations through the body are embodied ways of sensing music.

  • Embedded: Musical experience is shaped by its context. Our interactions with sound depend on the physical and social setting: the acoustics of a concert hall, the presence of other perceivers, and the cultural frame. These factors embed musical cognition in a wider world.

  • Enactive: We take an active part in making musical meaning. Listening is not passive; we anticipate, move, and respond, co-constructing the experience. A performer’s gestures or a perceiver’s dance are forms of enactive engagement.

  • Extended: Tools and technologies can extend our cognitive processes. Instruments, recording devices, and even smartphones become part of the system through which we sense, produce, and understand music.

The 4E perspective treats music as an interactive process that involves the whole person, situated in a specific context, working with both physical and digital tools.

Dig deeper: criticisms of 4E cognition

The 4E cognition framework is still in development and has also drawn criticism, some of which is collected in the Oxford Handbook of 4E Cognition Newen et al., 2018. One concern is that the definitions of “embodied,” “embedded,” “enactive,” and “extended” can be vague or overlapping. This makes 4E hard to tell apart from traditional cognitive science in practice.

Another criticism is whether there is enough empirical evidence for every part of the framework, especially the claim that cognition can be “extended” into tools or the environment. Critics also warn against overreach, granting cognitive status to objects such as smartphones or instruments that may not really take part in cognition. Some argue that 4E approaches downplay the neural mechanisms that traditional neuroscience emphasises. And there is lasting philosophical disagreement over whether 4E is a radical break or a reframing of older ideas about mind, body, and environment.

Even so, the 4E cognition framework has prompted lively interdisciplinary debate and new research in music cognition and neighbouring fields.

Movement, motion, action, and gesture¶

Before going further, we need to sort out four words that are often used interchangeably in everyday speech and even in the research literature: movement, motion, action, and gesture. Researchers in musicology, gesture studies, human–computer interaction, and human movement science all use these terms, but often with different meanings. Does a “piano gesture” refer to physical motion, the experience of an action, a sonic phrase, or something else? In this course, we follow the terminology developed in Sound Actions Jensenius, 2022:

  • Movement is the experienced phenomenon: the continuous excursions of the body, as we feel and perceive them. It is the everyday, experiential term. We talk about the “movement of a pianist’s hand” when we mean how we sense the hand travelling through the air.

  • Motion is the physics of movement: the continuous displacement of an object in time and space. Motion can be measured objectively, for example with a motion capture system, and described with the kinematic quantities introduced later in this chapter (position, velocity, acceleration). Motion is continuous, with no beginning or end, so it does not make sense to talk about “a motion.”

  • Action is the subjective experience of a movement sequence. It can be represented as a chunk of motion with a fuzzy beginning and end. Actions are often goal-directed, but not always. Think of a drummer performing a stroke: you easily perceive it as one coherent action (lift, hit, return to rest), but the motion capture data will only show a continuous position signal. Where the action begins and ends is a matter of interpretation; nothing in the data itself marks the boundaries.

  • Gesture denotes the meaning-bearing components of actions. A gesture is not just physical motion or a goal-directed action; it is part of human communication, what the gesture researcher Adam Kendon calls “visible action as utterance.” Consider waving goodbye: you can wave with the left hand or the right, and the physical motion will differ, but the meaning, and hence the gesture, is the same.

Different languages

Norwegian has only one word, bevegelse, for both movement and motion, and so do Spanish (movimiento) and many other languages. The distinction therefore has to be made on purpose: we say movement for what we experience and motion for what we measure.

A matching three-way split applies to the body at rest: position is measured, posture is how a person holds the body, and pose is a posture that carries meaning.

Dig deeper: position, posture, and pose

  • Position is the technical term: the physical location of a body, a body part, or an object in space. Like motion, it can be measured with a motion capture system, giving, say, the XYZ coordinates of a foot in the capture space at a given time.

  • Posture is the subjective term for how a person holds their body. It may be possible to calculate spatial features of a posture from position data, but that requires a subjective selection of which points to consider. Posture relates to position the way action relates to motion.

  • Pose refers to a posture with a meaning-bearing component: communication without moving. A pose is like a gesture, except that gestures are dynamic while poses are static. Lifting your hand to show a “thumbs up” is a gesture; holding the thumbs-up as a static sign is a pose.

The most important distinction, in both triads, is between what can be measured and what must be inferred. Motion, position, and force can be captured with sensors and cameras; actions, gestures, postures, and poses can only be interpreted from those measurements. A “gesture recognition” or “pose recognition” system therefore has to start by recording motion or position, segment the continuous data stream into actions or postures, and finally try to infer their meaning-bearing components.

The body in performance¶

When we talk about music-related body motion, it helps to separate performers from perceivers. Both depend on the body to engage with music, but their roles differ. This section looks at performers: the actions they make, how they time them, and how they coordinate with each other. The next section turns to perceivers.

Sound-producing and other actions¶

Music performance is physical. Musicians use coordinated actions, chunks of motion directed at a musical goal, to produce sound, shape phrases, and communicate with others. The body is both the source of musical ideas and their interpreter, turning intention into audible and visible action. This holds for all music-making: singing, playing instruments, conducting, and dancing.

We can sort music-related body motion in performers into four main types:

  • Sound-producing actions: Movements that directly generate sound on an instrument or interface. They include selection (choosing notes or sounds), excitation (initiating sound by striking, plucking, or bowing), and modification (altering qualities such as pitch or timbre). Examples: pressing piano keys, bowing a violin, turning a synthesiser knob. These actions are the performer’s half of the action–sound couplings introduced in the acoustics chapter; on electronic instruments they instead drive designed action–sound mappings, which we discussed in the electroacoustics chapter.

  • Sound-facilitating actions: Movements that support sound production without making sound themselves. Examples: a pianist’s arm movement for dynamics, a clarinettist’s breath support, tapping a foot to keep time.

  • Sound-accompanying actions: Motion that reflects or mimics musical features without producing sound, such as tracing sound contours in the air, mimicking sound-producing actions. Examples: moving a hand upward with rising pitch, playing air guitar, dancing to music.

  • Communicative actions: Actions that convey meaning, emotion, or instructions to other performers or the audience. They may be expressive, regulatory, or linguistic. Examples: a conductor’s baton movements, a nod to cue an entrance, expressive hand gestures.

The categories are useful for clarity, but in practice they overlap. Sound-facilitating actions are often inseparable from sound-producing ones, and performers may use communicative actions while playing. Keeping this interplay in view matters when studying music-related body motion.

In performance these actions are not produced one at a time. Neighbouring actions blend: a pianist’s hand is already shaping the next chord while it plays the current one, and a drummer’s stroke begins as the previous one ends. This is coarticulation, a term borrowed from phonetics, where the mouth prepares the next speech sound before the current one has ended Godøy et al., 2010. It is one reason the boundaries between actions are a matter of interpretation rather than something a recording marks on its own, which is the distinction the previous section drew between what can be measured and what must be inferred.

Producing rhythm: timing in the body¶

In the Time and rhythm chapter, we treated a beat mostly as something heard. However, the body is also central in beat perception. The two exercises in that chapter had you tapping, first at your own rate and then along with a click. These are small experiments in rhythm production.

Tapping a finger in time with a metronome is an example of sensorimotor synchronisation Repp, 2005. Two findings recur across hundreds of studies. First, taps do not fall on the clicks but slightly before them, by 20–50 ms for most people and by less for trained musicians. This negative mean asynchrony is a sign that tapping is prediction rather than reaction. A reaction to a sound takes at least 150 ms, far longer than the gap, so the tap must have been planned to coincide with a click that had not yet sounded. Second, the taps vary. The standard deviation of the asynchronies is typically 3–5 % of the interval, so at 120 BPM a tapper is “on the beat” only within a window of some 15–25 ms. The width of that window is roughly what separates a drummer from the rest of us.

Dig deeper: the two-level timing model

Where does the variability come from? A classic experiment lets the metronome stop and asks the tapper to continue at the same rate. The intervals between the continuation taps drift and jitter. Their pattern of correlations reveals two separate sources of noise: an internal timekeeper that decides when the next tap is due, and the motor delay between that decision and the finger hitting the table, the two-level timing model Wing & Kristofferson, 1973. The clock is in the head and the hand is late by a variable amount.

Timing is not confined to the finger. People bounce, nod, sway, and step to music at rates around 2 Hz (120 BPM), the same range as the spontaneous motor tempo introduced in the rhythm chapter. Moving also helps music perception. Participants who tapped along with a rhythm were better at hearing whether a later note was on time than participants who sat still Manning & Schutz, 2013. That is one reason drummers and dancers keep the whole body in play rather than just the hand that strikes.

For performers, timing and sound are produced together. When drummers were asked to play the same groove laid-back, on the beat, and pushed, their strokes moved by tens of milliseconds as instructed, but their sound changed too. Laid-back strokes were played louder and with longer decays, so the timing style was audible in the timbre and not only in the onset Câmara et al., 2020.

The body that produces a rhythm also shapes it, which is why, in the microrhythm section of time and rhythm, a chord’s perceived onset moved with how it was struck. The reverse coupling holds for perceivers, as the motion capture study in the rhythm chapter showed: bodies followed beats shifted by a few tens of milliseconds that the perceivers never noticed Danielsen et al., 2015.

The measurement side follows the scale of the motion. Finger and foot timing are captured by tapping apps and sensors, such as the Spontaneous tempo and Tap along apps of the rhythm chapter, and whole-body timing by the motion capture methods described later in this chapter.

Playing together: joint action¶

The term joint action refers to two or more people aligning perception and movement towards a shared goal in time: locking to the same pulse in an ensemble, adjusting attacks to a conductor’s gesture, breathing with a choir, or coupling dance steps to a metre. The mechanism underneath is the entrainment of coupled oscillators described in time and rhythm. Cognitive scientists sometimes separate shared intentionality (we mean to play this phrase together) from plain interpersonal synchrony (our movements happen to coincide). Both turn up in rehearsal and performance.

Coordination draws on several senses at once. Sound can be enough, but visual cues such as conducting, head nods, and instrument motion, and even touch or closeness, all help. Laura Bishop and colleagues have measured this in duos and ensembles. Cueing gestures such as head nods communicate both where the next beat falls and the intended tempo Bishop & Goebl, 2018, and in duos, movements that communicate alternate with movements that simply interact with the partner’s Bishop et al., 2019.

Roles matter too. Leader–follower relationships and deliberate asynchrony, such as a jazz soloist laying back behind the beat or a band pushing forward, are musically meaningful, not errors. Musical joint action needs constant error correction, the small delays and anticipations that keep parts distinct yet coordinated.

Research on joint action links synchrony to social cohesion and to feeling. The presence of others (social facilitation), the awareness of being judged (evaluation apprehension), and shared excitement can all change movement and physiology. Felt togetherness and measured coordination do not always agree, however. In piano duos, what the players attended to changed both how together they felt and how interactive their body motion was Bishop, 2025. Togetherness can even survive distance. In networked music and dance performances between two sites, performers and audiences describe a shared presence that the researchers call performative togetherness Serdar et al., 2026.

The body in perception¶

Many of the same actions show up in perceivers, people “listening” to music. We put “listen” in quotation marks because we experience music with the whole body, which is the heart of embodied music cognition. Perceivers also make sounds during a performance, whether involuntary (breathing) or voluntary (clapping, singing along).

Moving to music¶

Perceiver movements can be voluntary or involuntary, and they shape how we experience and understand music. Examples include:

  • Dancing: Whole-body rhythmic movement, structured as in ballroom or folk dance or improvised; the most direct way to embody musical structure, rhythm, and emotion, and a part of every musical culture.
  • Air performance: Imitating the actions of playing or singing, as in “air guitar” or lip-syncing, which lets a perceiver simulate being the performer.
  • Voluntary tapping or stamping: Bodily responses that keep the perceiver synchronised with the beat; finger-tapping is the standard laboratory measure of beat perception, as the previous section showed.
  • Involuntary swaying or nodding: Subtle motion without conscious intent, seen across cultures and ages, which shows how closely the auditory and motor systems are coupled.

These bodily responses are not mere by-products of listening; they are part of musical perception and cognition. Moving to music can strengthen memory, emotional response, and social connection.

Dance couples full-body movement with musical time, metre, and phrase structure in culturally specific ways. Foot patterns, spine shape, and use of space are not decorative add-ons; they are part of how rhythm and identity are expressed. Ethnochoreology and dance studies document repertoires where “the beat” lives as much in stride and bounce as in the drums.

Why we move¶

There is no single reason why we move to music, but research points to three that work together.

  • Coupling: Hearing a regular pulse activates motor areas of the brain, so the body is primed to synchronise with it through entrainment. Few other animals do this. The cockatoo Snowball became famous for bobbing his head to pop songs and staying with the beat when the tempo was changed Patel et al., 2009, but such cases are rare.
  • Pleasure: Moving to music is rewarding. People report the strongest urge to move, and the most pleasure, when a rhythm has a medium amount of syncopation, neither too simple nor too complex Witek et al., 2014. The reward system behind this is covered in the brain.
  • Bonding: Moving together in time tends to make people feel closer to each other, which is one reason why dancing is a social activity in so many cultures.

Micromotion¶

Does music really make people move? That has been an active research topic at the University of Oslo through the standstill paradigm: instead of asking people to move to music, we have asked them to stand as still as possible, and measured what happens when music is played.

It is impossible to stand absolutely still, and the remaining micromotion can be used to study the effects of music. Through the Championships of Standstill, we have measured more than 700 people standing still for 5–10 minutes at a time, with alternating music and silence. Everyone moves, at around 5 mm/s of head sway. The amount is similar from person to person and steady over time. The numbers are small, but, on average, listening to music makes people move a little more than standing in silence. Electronic dance music moved people most, and complex drum patterns (but not too complex) moved them more than a plain beat. Also, most motion came at around 120 BPM, in accordance with the spontaneous motor tempo.

Looking for individual differences, we found that younger participants moved slightly more than older ones. Height and gender made no difference. However, people scoring high in “empathic concern” moved more to music than others Zelechowska et al., 2020, and listening over headphones produced more head motion than the same music over loudspeakers Zelechowska et al., 2020.

Conversely, when analysing the swaying of audiences at a classical music concert, we found a stilling response: when the music turned quiet or paused, the audience’s motion dropped with it, as if the perceivers anticipated the stillness together Upham et al., 2024. Music can also make people stiller than they would otherwise be. So to conclude, music does not always make people move.

Feeling sound through the body¶

Motion is only one way of moving to music. Haptics covers touch and forces, and it overlaps with passive tactile vibration for musicians: fingering contact on strings or keys, stick rebound, and the resonance of an instrument body against the torso. Sound also reaches the skin directly. At a concert, the low-frequency energy of bass and kick drum radiates through floors and bodies, so you feel the drop in your chest as much as you hear it.

The percussionist Evelyn Glennie, who lost most of her hearing by the age of twelve, has built a world-class career based on haptics. She learned to feel vibrations through her hands, feet, and body, often performing barefoot, and uses that information to play and interpret music, as she describes in her TED talk How to truly listen:

Vibrotactile feedback is nowadays also being implemented in digital musical instruments. This makes it possible for performers to feel vibrations like in an acoustic instrument. Accessibility-oriented concert designs for Deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences route timing and pulse through vibrating platforms or wearables. Together with hearing—and vision when available—these channels make up multimodal musical engagement.

Anatomy and biomechanics¶

This section briefly introduces some terms from anatomy and biomechanics so that it is easier to understand empirical papers on music and movement.

Planes, axes, and joints¶

When describing the human body, it is common to start from the anatomical position: standing upright, eyes forward, arms at the sides with the palms facing forward. From there, three planes divide the body and three axes run through it. The sagittal plane separates left from right, the frontal plane front from back, and the transverse plane upper from lower, and the axes run side to side, front to back, and up and down. A nod is a rotation in the sagittal plane; a shake of the head is one in the transverse plane; swaying to music is mostly motion along the side-to-side axis.

Figure: The three anatomical planes: sagittal (left from right), frontal (front from back), and transverse (upper from lower).

Joints are where the skeleton moves, and their motion is named in pairs, always from the anatomical position: flexion and extension in the sagittal plane, abduction and adduction in the frontal plane, and internal and external rotation in the transverse plane.

Each joint allows a certain number of motion directions, which is its number of degrees of freedom, and each has a range of motion, measured in degrees of angle. A wrist has more freedom than a knee, and a pianist’s reach is a question of range.

Figure: The main joint movements, named in pairs relative to the anatomical position.

Dig deeper: muscles and bones

The musculoskeletal system has two parts: the skeleton, about 206 bones that act as levers and give muscles their attachment points, joined at the joints; and some 600 skeletal muscles, each a muscle belly with a tendon at either end that pulls on a bone when the muscle contracts. Muscles can only pull, never push, so every movement is a collaboration: the agonist drives it, synergists assist and stabilise, and the antagonist produces the opposite action so that the movement stays controlled. This is why a fast drum stroke is as much a matter of releasing the antagonist as of driving the agonist, and why electromyography, which we will look at in physiology, can read the timing of a movement from the skin above the muscle before the limb has visibly moved. The other two kinds of muscle, cardiac and smooth, run the heart and the organs and belong to that chapter.

Kinematics and kinetics¶

Biomechanics applies mechanics to the body, and splits the study of a moving body in two: kinematics and kinetics.

Kinematics describes the motion itself, what moves, where, and how fast, without asking about the forces. A motion capture system records kinematics, a stream of positions over time from which velocity and acceleration are derived. Motion capture positions are typically described within a reference frame: a global one fixed to the room or stage, or a local one fixed to a body part, such as the hand relative to the forearm.

Kinetics describes what caused motion and is measured through force plates and pressure sensors. It can also be estimated from kinematics if you have a model of the body.

Dig deeper: kinematic and kinetic terms
  • Position: The location of a point or segment in space, usually as X, Y, Z coordinates.
  • Displacement and distance: The straight-line change in position from start to end, with a direction, against the total length of the path travelled.
  • Speed and velocity: How fast something moves, without and with a direction.
  • Acceleration: The rate at which velocity changes, how quickly something speeds up or slows down.
  • Force: Any push or pull that can change a body’s motion; muscles generate internal forces, and gravity, the ground, and the instrument supply external ones.
  • Torque: A rotational force that turns a body segment around a joint; muscles create torque to move limbs and hold posture.
  • Power: The rate at which work is done, which is what a drum stroke or a bow change spends.
  • Centre of gravity and base of support: The point where the body’s mass balances, and the area beneath the body, usually between the feet, that keeps it upright; a body stays balanced as long as the line of gravity from the one falls within the other.

Kinematic analysis lets us track motion patterns in performance, such as the path of a violinist’s bow or a pianist’s hand. Kinetics helps us understand how musicians generate and control movement, which informs both injury prevention and performance.

Motion capture¶

There are many ways to study human body motion. Many people picture “motion capture” as suits, markers, or sensors, but the term can cover any method that systematically records movement, qualitative as well as quantitative. In practice the two are often combined. Researchers may use both video and sensors, and an analysis may mix statistics and interpretation. For clarity, this course treats qualitative and quantitative methods separately, while recognising that mixed-method approaches are common.

Writing about a motion study

When you summarise or report a motion capture study, keep the chapter’s vocabulary: motion for what the markers, sensors and cameras measured (quantity of motion, periodicity, posture, sway), movement for what the performer or perceiver experienced, gesture only when meaning is claimed. Say how many people were recruited and how many were analysed, name the device and its sampling rate, and name the model that was run on the numbers (a t-test, a linear mixed model, a periodicity analysis), since the analysis is part of the method. The people are participants, and a result supports or weakens a claim rather than proving it.

Qualitative approaches¶

Qualitative motion analysis works through observation, reflection, and descriptive frameworks rather than numerical measurement.

  • Introspection: Reflecting on one’s own movement experience. Musicians and researchers may assess their own performance, notice sensations of effort or discomfort, or describe how a movement feels during music-making.
  • Observation: Systematically watching others move—live or on video—and annotating how they move: timing, expressiveness, and so on. This can be a structured, repeatable way to document movement without specialised technology.

Observation-based methods are widely used in clinical, educational, sports, and artistic settings. Watching with the naked eye can be supplemented by video recordings that allow repeated viewing, slow-motion analysis, and collaborative review, which makes subtle details and patterns easier to spot.

Music researchers have drawn on the qualitative methods developed by the dancer and choreographer Rudolf Laban (1879–1958), who created two influential systems in the early to mid-20th century: Labanotation and Laban Movement Analysis (LMA).

Dig deeper: Laban’s two systems
  • Labanotation: A symbolic notation system for recording and analysing human movement, especially in dance. It uses standardised symbols for body parts, directions, levels, and timing, allowing detailed documentation of movement sequences.

Figure: Three sequences in Labanotation, read from the bottom up: a) walking forwards; b) walking forwards on the toes, then backwards; c) the right side of the body moving forwards and then to the right, the left side forwards and then to the left. From Jensenius (2009).

  • Laban Movement Analysis (LMA): A framework for describing the qualitative side of movement. LMA covers four components: body (what moves), effort (how it moves), shape (the form the body takes), and space (where it moves). The “effort” component is especially relevant to music, describing motion in terms of space (direct/indirect), time (sudden/sustained), weight (strong/light), and flow (bound/free); a punch, for example, is direct, sudden, and strong.

Marker-based motion capture¶

The reference standard in movement research is optical, marker-based motion capture. Small reflective markers are attached to the body, and a ring of cameras around the room, typically 8–20, films the scene in infrared light at 100–500 frames per second. Each camera sees the markers as bright dots in its own two-dimensional image. Because the positions of the cameras are known from a calibration with a wand of known length, the dots can be combined into three-dimensional positions with a precision well under a millimetre. The result is the position of each marker over time, in the global frame of the room, from which velocity, acceleration, joint angles, and distances between performers can be derived.

While a marker-based system gives the highest precision and accuracy, it requires a controlled setting, such as a lab, and the placement of reflective markers on the points that one wants to capture.

Sensor-based motion capture¶

The workhorse of sensor-based motion capture is the inertial measurement unit (IMU). There is one in every phone and smartwatch and in most wearable research devices. It combines three sensors. An accelerometer measures acceleration, including the constant pull of gravity. A gyroscope measures how fast the unit turns. A magnetometer, often included, gives its heading relative to the Earth’s magnetic field. Combining the three, which is called sensor fusion, gives the orientation of the unit at every moment.

Dig deeper: why an IMU cannot give position

An IMU does not give position. Acceleration has to be integrated twice to get position, and the small errors in each sample add up quickly, so a path reconstructed from an IMU alone drifts within seconds. An IMU also measures in its own local frame: the acceleration it reports is along the axes of the chip, wherever the chip happens to point, and converting that to the global frame of the room takes the orientation estimate and its errors with it. In practice, sensor data are at their best for what happens at the sensor, such as the timing and sharpness of a stroke, the rate of a sway, or the orientation of a head, and camera data for where things are.

The strengths are portability and independence from the surroundings, which suit field studies, concerts, and places where cameras are impractical. Sensors can also be combined with other signals on one clock. Chest-worn research devices such as the Equivital carry an IMU together with ECG, respiration, and skin-temperature sensors, so that a performer’s or perceiver’s motion, heart rate, and breathing are recorded as one data set, a combination we return to in the physiology chapter. The costs are careful placement and calibration, drift, and interference from nearby metal and electronics.

Video analysis¶

Ordinary video sits between the qualitative and quantitative approaches described above. Watching and annotating a recording is a qualitative method. However, it is possible to use computational tools to extract features from the video stream and show them as images and curves or estimate the positions of body parts the way a marker system does.

Different video cameras¶

An ordinary camera, in a phone, a webcam, or a camcorder, records the light reflected from the scene at 25–60 frames per second. But there are many other types of cameras.

A depth camera, such as the Microsoft Kinect and its successors, adds a distance for every pixel, measured by projecting an infrared pattern or by timing a pulse of light, so that a body stands out from its background by distance rather than by colour.

A thermal camera records the infrared light that warm bodies emit rather than the light they reflect, so people appear as bright shapes in any lighting, including darkness, and faces are not recognisable, which makes the recordings anonymous by construction; the physiology chapter uses one to read skin temperature across a whole audience.

A 360-degree camera stitches two or more fisheye lenses into a full sphere. These videos can also be “flattened” to look like a regular video recording.

There are also various types of industrial cameras that record with multiple lenses next to each other and high-speed cameras that can record hundreds or thousands of frames per second.

From frames to motion¶

A video is a series of still images, called frames. The simplest way to find motion in it is frame differencing: each frame is subtracted from the one before, pixel by pixel, and only the size of the difference is kept. Pixels that did not change come out black, and pixels where something moved come out bright. The result is a motion image, which shows where the motion is and hides everything that stands still: the background, the furniture, and any part of the body at rest. A small threshold removes the flicker and sensor noise that would otherwise make every pixel change a little.

Two numbers summarise a motion image. The quantity of motion is the share of pixels that changed, a rough measure of how much is moving. The centroid of motion is the average position of those pixels, a rough measure of where the motion happens. Both can be plotted over time like any other signal, and a plot of quantity of motion is often the first thing to look at in a recording of a performance or a dance. They are measures of the image, not of the body: a person moving towards the camera changes fewer pixels than one moving sideways, and a change in the lighting counts as motion too.

Videograms and motiongrams¶

A plot of quantity of motion reduces each frame to one number and loses where in the image things happened. A videogram keeps one dimension of the image. Each frame is collapsed into a single column by averaging the pixels along each row, and the columns are placed side by side over time Jensenius, 2013. Time then runs from left to right and the height of the frame from top to bottom, so a hand moving up and down draws a wave. A motiongram is made in the same way from the motion images, so it shows only what moved. Averaging along each column instead gives a horizontal version, which shows sideways motion.

A motiongram can be read much like a spectrogram, with time along one axis, but with position in the image instead of frequency along the other. A self-similarity matrix takes a different route: it compares every frame with every other frame and colours each pair by how alike they are. Repeated movements then show up as a grid of stripes, and a change of section as a change of texture, just as for audio in machine listening.

Video and sound on one timeline¶

Many questions about music-related movement concern how the movement relates to the sound: whether a nod lands on the beat, whether a bow stroke starts before the note, or how a dancer’s motion follows the music. Answering them needs the video and the audio on the same time axis. The Video scrubber app does this for a recording. It draws a videogram or motiongram of the video above a spectrogram of its sound, and clicking anywhere on either jumps the video to that moment. You can load a video file from your own computer or record a short clip with the camera and microphone; either way, the recording stays in the browser.

Markerless motion capture¶

The visualisations above work directly on the pixels and say nothing about which pixel belongs to which body part. Markerless (or marker-free) motion capture estimates the body’s joints from ordinary video. The task is called pose estimation, and it uses computer vision models trained on large sets of images in which people’s joints were labelled by hand. The output is a skeleton: the positions of a few dozen landmarks, such as wrists, elbows, and the tip of the nose, in every frame. One camera gives the skeleton in two dimensions; two or three calibrated cameras are needed for three.

Markerless capture still has practical limits. Occlusion (one limb hiding another), loose clothing, uneven lighting, and poor finger detail can reduce accuracy compared with marker-based or sensor-based systems. For many music studies it is nonetheless a practical entry point before investing in a lab.

Going deeper: markerless motion-capture tools

Several open libraries and research pipelines illustrate what is possible today:

  • MediaPipe: Google’s framework for real-time 2D pose, hand, and face tracking from a webcam or phone. Widely used in teaching and prototyping, since it runs on ordinary hardware with little setup.
  • OpenPose: a landmark system from Carnegie Mellon for multi-person 2D body, hand, and foot keypoints. Influential in research and still a common baseline.
  • FreeMoCap: an open-source pipeline with a graphical interface for research-grade 3D markerless capture from one or more webcams, or from imported video. It calibrates the capture volume, processes the recordings, and exports kinematic data. Aimed at labs and classrooms that want multi-camera 3D without marker suits (documentation).
  • MAMMA: Markerless Accurate Multi-person Motion Acquisition, a research pipeline from the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems that recovers detailed 3D body models (SMPL-X) from multi-view video, including two people interacting Cuevas Velasquez et al., 2026. Relevant for joint musical action, such as duets, ensemble choreography, or conductor–orchestra coupling, where more than one person must be tracked in 3D.

Other camera-based options are depth sensors (for example Azure Kinect body tracking), smartphone pipelines such as OpenCap, and commercial markerless services. The tools above are among the most accessible open starting points.

Sonification: hearing motion¶

Sonification is a technique that turns data into sound Hermann et al., 2011. Hearing is good at following changes over time and at picking out rhythm and regularity, so a sonification can reveal a periodic pattern or a sudden change that is easy to miss in a visualisation (for example, a plot), and it leaves the eyes free to watch the performer.

A critical look: does the right playlist make you run faster?¶

Music has been called a legal performance-enhancing drug. Many people use music during exercise, but race organisers typically ban using music in competitions.

  • The claim is that music makes people exercise harder, longer, or faster, partly by distracting from fatigue and partly by pulling movement into step with the beat.
  • The evidence was gathered in a meta-analysis of 139 studies with around 3,600 participants Terry et al., 2020. Music reliably improved mood during exercise, lowered perceived exertion, and improved physical performance measures such as distance covered and time to exhaustion, with a small effect on the efficiency of oxygen use. The performance benefit was moderate at most, was largest for low-to-moderate intensities, and was greater when the music’s tempo was matched to the movement, which is the entrainment mechanism from earlier in this chapter at work.
  • The method is mostly short laboratory bouts on treadmills and ergometers with self-selected or prescribed music, compared with silence or noise. Few studies involve trained athletes at competitive intensities.
  • The limits follow from that. The reduction in perceived effort fades as intensity rises, since attention narrows to the body once breathing gets hard; the performance effects are averages over many small studies; and mood is measured more consistently than speed.

Music does move the body, and the effect is measurable. But it is modest, it works through attention and mood as much as through the beat, and it is strongest when the exercise is comfortable enough for the music to be heard at all.

Chapter summary

This chapter followed music into the body. Embodied and 4E perspectives hold that music is perceived and made with the whole body, and the terms movement, motion, action, and gesture separate what a system can measure from what a person must infer. Performers act on their instruments with sound-producing, sound-facilitating, sound-accompanying, and communicative actions that blend into one another. They keep time by predicting the beat rather than reacting to it, and they coordinate through sound, sight, and breath. Perceivers move too, from dancing to the millimetres of sway that the standstill championships measure: hearing a beat couples to the motor system, moving is pleasurable, and moving together bonds. Sound also reaches the body through the skin, not only the ears. Measuring any of this needs a vocabulary of planes, joints, and kinematics, and a method: marker-based cameras for precision in the lab, inertial sensors for orientation and acceleration in the field, and ordinary video, from which frame differencing, motiongrams, pose estimation, and sonification extract motion without touching the body. A critical look at music in exercise found measurable but modest benefits, largest at moderate intensity and when movement locks to the tempo.
