Tips and tricks

Reading, writing, spectrograms, and the exam

This page is a companion for MUS2640, and most of it applies to university work in general. Dip back in when you plan a text or prepare for an exam.

Reading¶

Most of what you learn at university comes through reading, and most of what you are judged on is what you make of it.

Reading a research article¶

Researchers get through tens of articles a week by not reading them from the top. With the habits below you can get a good overview of an article in about 15 minutes:

  1. Title and abstract. The title is a one-sentence abstract; the abstract summarises the paper. A confusing or thin abstract is often a sign of a weak article, with exceptions.
  2. The venue. The journal or conference tells you the field, and so which methods and standards to expect: a music psychology journal, a music technology conference, a musicology journal and a general science journal publish and review different kinds of work.
  3. The authors and their order. Names you recognise from the course tell you what to expect. In empirical research the first author usually did the work and the last author led the group; the humanities often list authors alphabetically, so the position means less there.
  4. The affiliations. A conservatory, a psychology department, a computer science lab and a company bring different questions, equipment and interests to the same topic.
  5. The figures. Browse every figure and caption before reading any prose. They often show the setup, the stimuli and the main result more directly than the text.
  6. Then the sections you need. If the paper is not for you, move on. If it is, use the IMRAD order (introduction, methods, results, discussion): the introduction for the question, the discussion for the answer, and the methods and results when you need to know how the answer was reached.
  7. Be critical. Use the checklist from the introduction, claim, evidence, method and limits, and the habits in the next section.
  8. Keep what you read. A reference manager such as Zotero stores PDFs, notes and citations, and saves time at every later stage.

Reading critically¶

When asked what the authors could have done differently, go through five levers: who took part, what they heard or did, what was measured, where it happened, and how the data were analysed. Propose one change on one lever and say what it would show that the study could not.

Name a limitation together with the inference it blocks: “fifteen trained singers” matters because it limits the power to find small effects and the generalisation to amateurs; “small sample” alone earns nothing. Keep “the authors found” apart from “I think”. Report a finding with its direction (closer, not different), and keep a measured association (singers in canon stood closer to the stand) apart from its explanation (more cognitive effort); say which you are offering. “No significant effect” means that the study found no evidence of one, not that the thing does not matter.

When you summarise a study, first write one sentence a reader could draw: who, how many, doing what, with which apparatus, measuring what, and check it against the method section. A single singer with a loudspeaker partner is not a choir, and twelve markers are not a motion-capture suit. Paraphrase each measure in your own words with its unit; pasting the authors’ list of variables shows that you found it, not that you understood it. Take the authors’ names from the article, not from the page that hosts it. When a question asks for the difference between two terms the article defines, quote the definitions with page numbers first, then say what you think.

Writing¶

Before you start¶

Read the task (compare, explain, argue, reflect), the word limit, the deadline, the format and the citation style before you draft, and ask rather than assume if anything is unclear.

Write the question before anything else, and end by answering it in the same words. “This text compares the rehearsal room and the hall” is a topic; “Does the longer reverberation of the hall change how a drummer times the next stroke?” is a question a reader can watch you answer. Most assignments that read as description rather than analysis never had a question.

Plan in a few bullet points, separating what something is from why it matters, and decide for each section which evidence you will lean on: articles, listening examples or your own data.

Structure and voice¶

An introduction gives the context, the question and a one- or two-sentence summary of the answer. Each body paragraph carries one idea: a topic sentence, the evidence, and a bridge to the next paragraph. The conclusion draws the layers together, connects to the introduction, and may note the limits of the argument or what further work would be needed.

Third person fits analysis (“The results suggest...”); first person is natural for reflection or methods when the assignment invites it. Prefer active phrasing when it clarifies who did what, and do not drift between voices. Write as to a stranger who will judge the text on its own: no contractions, no exclamation marks, no “not much to say here”. Informal asides are the most common register slip in this course’s assignments.

Writing about sound and music¶

Connect the text to the sound. Describe it in words, add a visualisation such as a spectrogram, and link to a recording with an exact address: “at 1:42 in the Coltrane recording”, or bar numbers against a named edition. “Later in the piece” is not an address.

Describe before you interpret. Say what happens in terms someone could verify by listening, then say what it means. “The saxophone enters an octave above the previous phrase, and the bass drops out for four bars, which leaves the melody exposed at the point where the text turns” earns its conclusion; “the music becomes emotional here” does not, because there is nothing in it to check.

Keep terminology fixed for the length of a text: pitches by letter (C4), by solfège or by scale degree; “tempo” as the notated marking or as the measured rate. Use the terms this book defines, such as timbre, metre and masking, in the book’s sense, and flag the everyday sense when you need it.

Every claim about sound carries a quantity and its unit: a reverberation time in seconds, a frequency in hertz, a level in decibels, a distance in metres. “Warm”, “rich” and “muddy” are starting points; the number is the evidence, cited from the place that measured it rather than from a textbook that discusses the topic.

Some pairs of words are confused in most years’ assignments, and the exam tests them:

  • Reverberation is many reflections that merge into a tail; an echo is one reflection you hear separately.
  • Level is measured in decibels; volume is a knob. Loudness is what you hear.
  • Absorption takes energy out of the sound and makes a room drier; diffusion scatters it; diffraction bends it round an edge or through a doorway; isolation (what people call soundproofing) keeps it out of the next room. Four words, four different things.
  • The horizontal and vertical dimensions of music are time and spectrum, the axes of the page and the spectrogram, not directions in a room.
  • A keynote is what you stop noticing, a sound signal is what makes you turn your head, a soundmark is what you would miss if it were gone.
  • Pitch is heard, frequency is measured; tempo is a rate, the beat is the level you tap.
  • Motion is what a sensor measures, movement is what a person experiences.
  • A listener only hears, a perceiver experiences music with the whole body.
  • Compression of data (MP3) and compression of dynamics (a compressor) share a word and nothing else.

A units check, since these slips recur: kHz is not Hz (an axis that ends at 22 kHz does not show an insect); 3 dB doubles the power, not the loudness; a logarithmic frequency axis is a property of the axis, not of the colours; sound travels at about 343 m/s in air, not 400.

When you work from your own analysis, a spectrogram, a tempo curve or a set of annotations, say how you produced it: which recording, which software, which settings. An analysis nobody can reproduce is an opinion with a figure attached.

Figures¶

A figure must be readable on its own, whether you made it or borrowed it.

  • Caption: says what the reader is looking at and what to notice, not a repeated title: “Spectrogram of a saxophone tone; note the evenly spaced harmonics”.
  • Axes, legend and scale: both axes labelled with units (Hz, dB, s, BPM); a legend when there is more than one series; the colour scale explained, and colour never the only carrier of meaning.
  • Readability: fonts large enough at the size the figure will appear, and nothing in the frame that does not need to be there.
  • Reference and credit: refer to each figure in the text (“Figure 3 shows...”) and cite the source if it is not your own.
  • A spectrogram is not a screenshot: crop to the plot and keep both axis scales. The caption states the sound, the window size, whether the frequency axis is linear or logarithmic, and what the colour scale measures, and then points at the thing to see with its frequency or time (“the second harmonic at 880 Hz fades after 1.2 s”). Figures labelled only “Linear” and “Log”, or placed at the end without being mentioned, were the most common weakness in two years of spectrogram assignments.
  • The sound itself: for every recording you analyse, give its author, a link and its licence, as for any other source.

Referencing¶

Reformulate other people’s thinking in your own words rather than quoting at length, and give page numbers when you cite or paraphrase something concrete. The reference list contains exactly the works cited, and nothing else. Cite the chapter or page of a book, the published source rather than a ResearchGate copy, and this textbook once, as a book, not each week’s page as a separate work. Any consistent style (APA, Chicago, Harvard) is accepted. Google Scholar is a good place to start searching; the university library gets you through the paywalls. Cite anything that is not common knowledge, including paraphrases and figures.

Before you submit¶

Three things decide whether a good text is read as one: that it answers the question that was asked and not a neighbouring one, that it has been revised, and that it arrives in the right form. Check the first against the brief, since every year a few essays answer a different question well. Revise in separate passes: structure (does the argument flow?), claims and evidence (is each point supported?), language and references (clarity, tone, accurate citation). Format consistently: font sizes, heading levels, indents and spacing.

Then the practical habits that save marks. Deliver a PDF named with the course code and the assignment number. Open every link in a private browser window before you submit: a Freesound upload still waiting for moderation, a profile page or a moderation queue is a dead link for the reader. Link to the sound’s own page as plain text and say what the file is (format, duration, device, microphone position, edits). Attach images, not the session file of the program that made them. Hand in on time. If an AI tool helped with the text or the analysis, say so where the rules ask for it, and keep the responsibility for every claim.

Spectrograms¶

Reading a spectrogram¶

A spectrogram is a picture of sound: time along the horizontal axis, frequency up the vertical axis, and brightness for how much energy the sound carries at each point. The assignments and the exam both test reading and making them.

The axes¶

The horizontal axis is time, as in a waveform display; a vertical slice of the image is one moment of sound.

The vertical axis is frequency in hertz, a physical quantity, not a note name. On a linear scale the distance from 0 to 1,000 Hz equals that from 1,000 to 2,000 Hz, which squeezes the musically important low range into a thin band at the bottom. On a logarithmic scale every octave gets the same space, so the step from 220 to 440 Hz looks as big as the step from 440 to 880 Hz. Check which scale you are looking at before interpreting anything.

The third dimension is colour or brightness: the energy at that time and frequency, usually in decibels. A spectrogram is a three-dimensional surface (time, frequency, energy) seen from above, with height turned into colour, so a bright spot at two seconds and 440 Hz means that at that moment the recording contained strong vibration at 440 cycles per second. A waveform shows only time and overall amplitude; the spectrogram unfolds the amplitude into its frequency content, which is why it shows more and takes more care to read.

One note is many lines¶

The most common misreading is to treat a spectrogram as a score or a piano roll. When one voice or instrument plays one note, the spectrogram shows a stack of horizontal lines, the partials of the tone, at whole-number multiples of the fundamental (see acoustics). One note at 220 Hz on a guitar gives lines at 220, 440, 660, 880 Hz and upwards. To an eye trained on piano rolls the stack looks like a chord; it is one note. The pitch you hear corresponds to the lowest line, and to the spacing between the lines; everything above shapes the timbre.

A spectrogram shows the physical, composite structure of sound, while a note is a perceptual unit: the auditory system fuses the stack, the spectrogram leaves it unfused for you to inspect. So follow the lowest strong line of a stack to find the melody, and expect the stacks of several instruments to interleave. Untangling them is the problem your auditory system solves in auditory scene analysis (psychoacoustics) and the reason automatic transcription remains hard (machine listening). When unsure whether a stack is one note or a chord, check the spacing: the partials of one harmonic tone are equally spaced in hertz (220, 440, 660), the fundamentals of a chord are not (a major triad on A has 220, 277 and 330 Hz, each with its own stack).

What common sounds look like¶

A harmonic sound, a sung vowel or a plucked string, is a stack of horizontal lines with the fundamental lowest. A drum hit or click is a thin vertical stripe: short in time, spread across all frequencies. Vibrato makes every line of a stack wave up and down, most visibly in the upper harmonics. Noise, a cymbal wash, wind or hiss, is a diffuse wash without lines. Silence is darkness, though background noise usually leaves a faint glow near the bottom. The figure below shows all of these in one synthesised clip.

# Annotated spectrogram of a synthesised clip with a count-in click,
# a harmonic tone with vibrato, a noise burst, and silence
import numpy as np
import librosa
import librosa.display
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.rcParams.update({
    "font.size": 10,
    "axes.titlesize": 11,
    "axes.labelsize": 10,
    "xtick.labelsize": 9,
    "ytick.labelsize": 9,
    "legend.fontsize": 9,
    "figure.figsize": (10, 4),
    "figure.dpi": 100,
    "savefig.dpi": 150,
    "axes.spines.top": False,
    "axes.spines.right": False,
})

rng = np.random.default_rng(2640)
sr = 22050
duration = 6.0
n = int(sr * duration)
t = np.linspace(0, duration, n, endpoint=False)
y = np.zeros(n)

# Count-in clicks: two short broadband bursts
for start in (0.4, 0.9):
    idx = (t >= start) & (t < start + 0.012)
    y[idx] += 0.9 * rng.uniform(-1, 1, idx.sum())

# Sustained harmonic tone with vibrato (f0 = 220 Hz, 5.5 Hz vibrato)
f0, vib_rate, vib_depth = 220.0, 5.5, 8.0
inst_f0 = f0 + vib_depth * np.sin(2 * np.pi * vib_rate * t)
phase0 = 2 * np.pi * np.cumsum(inst_f0) / sr
tone = np.zeros(n)
for k in range(1, 7):
    tone += (0.5 / k) * np.sin(k * phase0)
env = np.where((t >= 1.5) & (t < 3.8), 1.0, 0.0)
ramp = int(0.05 * sr)
attack = np.searchsorted(t, 1.5)
release = np.searchsorted(t, 3.8)
env[attack:attack + ramp] *= np.linspace(0, 1, ramp)
env[release - ramp:release] *= np.linspace(1, 0, ramp)
y += 0.6 * tone * env

# Noise burst
idx = (t >= 4.2) & (t < 4.8)
y[idx] += 0.35 * rng.uniform(-1, 1, idx.sum())

# Silence from 4.8 s onwards (nothing added)
y = y / np.max(np.abs(y))

n_fft, hop_length = 2048, 256
D = librosa.stft(y, n_fft=n_fft, hop_length=hop_length, window='hann')
S_db = librosa.amplitude_to_db(np.abs(D), ref=np.max)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4.2), constrained_layout=True)
img = librosa.display.specshow(S_db, sr=sr, hop_length=hop_length,
                               x_axis='time', y_axis='hz', cmap='magma', ax=ax)
ax.set_ylim(0, 2500)
ax.set_title('An annotated spectrogram of a six-second synthesised clip')
fig.colorbar(img, ax=ax, format='%+2.0f dB')

notes = [
    ('count-in clicks:\nvertical stripes', (0.65, 1900), (1.15, 2300)),
    ('harmonics:\nhorizontal stack', (2.6, 480), (2.05, 2050)),
    ('vibrato:\nwavy lines', (3.3, 1310), (3.85, 1850)),
    ('noise burst:\nbroadband wash', (4.5, 1500), (4.35, 2250)),
    ('silence:\ndarkness', (5.5, 900), (5.15, 1500)),
]
for text, xy, xytext in notes:
    ax.annotate(text, xy=xy, xytext=xytext, color='white',
                ha='center', va='center',
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->', color='white', lw=1.2))
plt.show()

Figure: An annotated spectrogram of a synthesised six-second clip: two count-in clicks, a sustained harmonic tone with vibrato, a noise burst and silence.

Every spectrogram is computed with an analysis window, and the window size sets a trade-off: a short window is sharp in time but blurry in frequency, a long window the opposite. Acoustics shows the same signal analysed with three window sizes.

Checklist when describing a spectrogram¶

  • The axes first: the time span, the frequency range, whether the frequency and intensity scales are linear or logarithmic, and the width of the colour scale (the dynamic range).
  • The settings: the window size (in samples or milliseconds) and the overlap, which decide what the picture can and cannot show.
  • The obvious events: where does something begin, end or change?
  • The content sorted into harmonic (horizontal lines), percussive (vertical stripes) and noisy (diffuse washes).
  • The development over time: what stays constant and what moves?
  • What you cannot conclude: a spectrogram shows physical energy, not perceived loudness, and does not by itself reveal which source produced the sound.

Making spectrograms in Sonic Visualiser¶

Sonic Visualiser is the tool used in this course. Open an audio file (File > Open) and you see a waveform; spectrograms are added as layers from the Layer menu, in three types computed from the same analysis but showing different things.

  • Regular spectrogram (Layer > Add Spectrogram, shortcut G): all frequencies from 0 Hz to half the sampling rate, energy as colour. Start here; it shows stacks, stripes and washes at once, but its linear frequency default gives most of the image to high frequencies where little music happens. The properties pane on the right holds the choices: Window sets the analysis window (1024 samples as a general default, 2048 or 4096 for sustained tones and fine pitch detail, 256 or 512 for drum hits and onsets), Scale sets the colour mapping (dB is usually the most readable), and the frequency axis can be switched between linear and logarithmic and zoomed with the wheels at the bottom right.
  • Melodic range spectrogram (shortcut M): the same analysis preset for melody, with a restricted range, a logarithmic axis and a longer window. Pitch contours, vibrato and slides read almost like a drawn line. Even here one sung note is several parallel lines, and the contour is the lowest strong one.
  • Peak frequency spectrogram (shortcut K): draws a thin line at the estimated exact frequency of the strongest component in each bin, so partials become needle-thin traces whose frequencies can be read off accurately. This is the view for tuning, exact harmonic frequencies, and whether two lines are really one; noise and reverberation come out speckled, since they really are smeared across frequency.

For an overview use the regular spectrogram, to follow melody the melodic range spectrogram, to measure a clear tonal sound the peak frequency spectrogram. Keep two of them as layers in one pane and switch between them with the tabs at the top of the properties pane; the differences teach you what each view hides.

Exporting a spectrogram as a figure¶

Do not hand in a screenshot of the window. Switch on the vertical scale and the time ruler in the View menu, use File > Export Image, and crop to the plot if you must. The window size, the overlap and the colour scale are not drawn into the image, so they go in the caption, as the figure habits above say.

Studying and the exam¶

Study technique¶

Most people learn more from short focused blocks (about 30 minutes) with real breaks than from hours in front of the same page, and from a calm place without digital distractions. After reading a chapter, close the notes and write half a page in your own words, explain a concept aloud as if to someone new to it, or turn each heading into a question and answer it before rereading. Since the course is about listening and experiencing your sonic environment, apply what you learn to what you hear every day. AI tools can help you rehearse; NotebookLM, for instance, can pose test questions on this textbook.

The exam¶

The exam is a school exam without aids, so you answer from memory, without notes or a computer. The questions test understanding of the main concepts and how they relate, not recall of detail. The best preparation is to read the textbook, come to class and do the assignments, and to sharpen your use of the course’s terms, from “sound” against “audio” to the word pairs above.

Three skills carry most of the marks and can be practised in advance:

  • Reading a spectrogram. Go through the checklist above on four different recordings until the axes, the scales and the window size are the first things you name. In an answer, keep a fixed order: channels, frequency axis (linear or logarithmic, range, unit), time axis (present or not, duration), colour map and decibel scale, then the events. Write each as an observation with its number, then infer the source and name the feature that supports it, then say which setting you would change to test the guess.
  • Using the terms precisely. Schafer’s keynote, sound signal and soundmark are mixed up in most years; keep the one-line test from listening in your head. When a question asks why machines struggle to hear as we do, answer from psychoacoustics, not from artificial intelligence: the ear is non-linear, with equal-loudness contours, masking, critical bands and just noticeable differences, and a machine has none of that unless it is built in.
  • Reading a study critically. The question of what a study could have done differently is where most marks are lost. Practise the five levers above on one article a week, and always propose one concrete change rather than a general complaint.

During the exam, answer every sub-question, in the order asked, with its letter as a label. A blank sub-question costs more than any wrong answer, and the strongest scripts give each part a full page rather than a paragraph. If time is short, write three sentences for each sub-question first, then expand. If a question freezes you, begin with what you do know and return later.
