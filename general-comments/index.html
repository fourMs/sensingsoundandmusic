Tips and tricks

Reading, writing, spectrograms, and the exam

This page is a companion for MUS2640 but is probably helpful for university work in general. You do not need to read it all at once; dip back in when you are planning a text or coming up to an exam.

Reading¶

Most of what you learn at university comes through reading, and most of what you are judged on is what you make of it. This part is about reading research efficiently and reading it critically.

Reading a research article¶

As a student, you need to know how to read a research article quickly. Most researchers “read” tens or even hundreds of articles per day. This is part of how one navigates existing knowledge. One can use AI-based tools to help, but by applying some human tricks, it is possible to get a good overview of an article in around 15 minutes:

  1. Title and abstract. Start by reading the title and abstract. That is probably what brought you to the paper in the first place. The title often functions as a one-sentence “abstract” on its own, while the abstract itself summarises the main content of the paper. There are good and bad articles, and there are good and bad abstracts. A bad abstract (in the sense of being confusing or not containing enough information) can be an indication of a bad article, but there are exceptions.

  2. The journal or venue. Before reading any further, you should check the name of the journal and/or the publisher. This will tell you which field the article belongs to, and so which methods and standards to expect: a music psychology journal, a music technology conference, a musicology journal, and a general science journal publish different kinds of work and review them differently.

  3. The authors. Check whether you recognise any of the names from this course or from other readings. As you progress in your studies, you will get to know the work of various researchers and their collaborators (students, colleagues). These networks of researchers can give you an indication of what to expect.

  4. The order of the authors. Check the order of names in the author list. In empirical research the first author is usually the one who did the work, and the last author is often the senior researcher who led the group or supervised it. Other fields have other conventions, including alphabetical order, so the position of a name means less in the humanities than in the sciences.

  5. The affiliations. Where the authors work also gives a lead on context: a conservatory, a psychology department, a computer science lab, and a company bring different questions, equipment, and interests to the same topic.

  6. The figures. Before you start reading anything, browse through the paper and look at every figure and its caption. Figures often show the setup, the stimuli, and the main result more directly than the prose. So you can learn a lot about the study without reading any of the content.

  7. Then the sections you need. At this point, you may have realised that the paper is not interesting for you. Move on. If you are interested, then you do not necessarily need to read from the top. Many empirical articles follow the same order: introduction, method, results, analysis, and discussion (the “IMRAD formula”). Read the introduction for the question and the discussion for the answer, and go to the method and the results when you need to know how the answer was reached.

  8. Be critical. Use the checklist from the introduction, claim, evidence, method and limits, and the habits in the next section.

  9. Use a reference manager. Researchers usually use specific software to help with keeping track of papers and citations. A good, free solution is Zotero. There you can keep local copies of PDFs and write notes as you go.

Reading critically¶

When you are asked what the authors could have done differently, go through five levers: who took part, what they heard or did, what was measured, where it happened, and how the data were analysed. Suggest one change on one lever and say what it would show that the study could not.

Name a limitation only together with the inference it blocks: “fifteen trained singers” matters because it limits the power to find small effects and the generalisation to amateurs, and saying “small sample” alone earns nothing. Keep “the authors found” apart from “I think”, report a finding with its direction (closer, not different), and remember that a measured association (singers in canon stood closer to the stand) is not its explanation (more cognitive effort); say which of the two you are offering. “No significant effect” means that the study found no evidence of one, not that the thing does not matter.

When you summarise a study, first write one sentence a reader could draw: who, how many, doing what, with which apparatus, measuring what. Check it against the method section; a single singer with a loudspeaker partner is not a choir, and twelve markers are not a motion-capture suit. Then paraphrase each measure in your own words with its unit. Pasting the authors’ own list of variables shows that you found the list, not that you understood it. Take the authors’ names from the article, not from the web page that hosts it. And when a question asks for the difference between two terms the article defines, quote the article’s definitions with page numbers first, and only then say what you think.

Writing¶

Before you start¶

Good writing (academic and other) starts by having a clear idea of what you want to write about. If it is an assignment, the task is already given (compare, explain, argue, reflect, etc.). If not, you will need to decide yourself. You should also note word limits, deadline, format, and the citation style before you invest time in drafting. If anything is unclear, ask rather than assume something.

Write the question before you write anything else, and end by answering it in the same words. “This text compares the rehearsal room and the hall” is a topic; “Does the longer reverberation of the hall change how a drummer times the next stroke?” is a question a reader can watch you answer. Most assignments that read as description rather than analysis never had a question.

Planning saves time. Sketch a few bullet points for your main line of argument, then distinguish description from analysis, which means separating what something is from why it matters in context. For each section, know what evidence you will lean on: research articles, listening examples, or some of your own data.

Structure¶

A clear essay usually has an introduction that describes the context, the question you answer, and a one- or two-sentence summary. Then, each body paragraph should be built around one idea starting with a topic sentence, then some evidence backing up your argument, and finally a bridge forward to the next paragraph. Think of writing as creating an onion. You start with a core and develop layers that support that argument. You should end with a conclusion that knits the various threads you have woven together and connects to your introduction. You may briefly note limits of your argument or what further research would be needed.

Voice and register¶

For voice, third person often fits analysis (“The results suggest…”), while first person is natural for reflection or methods when the assignment invites it. Prefer active phrasing when it clarifies who did what, and avoid drifting between voices without reason. Write as you would to a stranger who will judge the text on its own: no contractions, no exclamation marks, no “not much to say here”. Informal asides are the most common register slip in this course’s assignments.

Writing about sound and music¶

Writing about sound and music poses some challenges with connecting to the material you are describing. You can describe the sound with words and you can include visualisations (such as a spectrogram). It often also helps to link to a recording, but then be careful with describing the location you are describing (“at 1:42 in the Coltrane recording”), or bar numbers against a named edition if you are working from a score. “Later in the piece” is not an address.

Then describe before you interpret. Say what happens in terms someone could verify by listening, and only then say what you think it means. “The saxophone enters an octave above the previous phrase, and the bass drops out for four bars, which leaves the melody exposed at the point where the text turns” earns its conclusion. “The music becomes emotional here” does not, because there is nothing in it to check.

Keep your terminology fixed for the length of a text. Decide whether you are naming pitches by letter (C4), by solfège, or by scale degree, and stay with it. Decide whether “tempo” in your essay means the notated marking or the measured rate, and say which. If you use a term this book defines, such as timbre, metre, or masking, use it in that sense rather than the loose everyday one, and if you need the everyday sense, flag it.

Every claim about sound should carry a quantity and its unit: a reverberation time in seconds, a frequency in hertz, a level in decibels, a distance in metres. “Warm”, “rich” and “muddy” are starting points; the number is the evidence. If the number comes from somewhere else, cite the place that measured it, not a textbook that happens to discuss the topic.

Some pairs of words are confused in most years’ assignments, and the exam tests them:

  • Reverberation is many reflections that merge into a tail; an echo is one reflection you hear separately.
  • Level is measured in decibels; volume is a knob. Loudness is what you hear.
  • Absorption takes energy out of the sound and makes a room drier; diffusion scatters it; diffraction bends it round an edge or through a doorway; isolation (what people call soundproofing) keeps it out of the next room. Four words, four different things.
  • The horizontal and vertical dimensions of music are time and spectrum, the axes of the page and the spectrogram, not directions in a room.
  • A keynote is what you stop noticing, a sound signal is what makes you turn your head, a soundmark is what you would miss if it were gone.
  • Pitch is heard, frequency is measured; tempo is a rate, the beat is the level you tap.
  • Motion is what a sensor measures, movement is what a person experiences
  • A listener only hears, a perceiver experiences music with the whole body.
  • Compression of data (MP3) and compression of dynamics (a compressor) share a word and nothing else.

And a units check, since these slips appear in exam scripts every year: kHz is not Hz (an axis that ends at 22 kHz does not show an insect); 3 dB doubles the power, not the loudness; a logarithmic frequency axis is a property of the axis, not of the colours; sound travels at about 343 m/s in air, not 400.

When you are working from your own analysis, whether that is a spectrogram, a tempo curve, or a set of annotations, describe how you produced it. Which recording, which software, which settings. An analysis nobody can reproduce is an opinion with a figure attached.

Remember to cite anything that is not common knowledge, including paraphrases, quotations, and figures.

Figures and plots¶

A figure should be readable on its own. Whether you make it yourself or borrow it, check that a reader could understand it without digging through your prose.

  • Caption: Every figure needs a caption that says what the reader is looking at and what to notice, not just a repeated title. A good caption names the data and points to the takeaway (for example, “Spectrogram of a saxophone tone; note the evenly spaced harmonics”).
  • Axes: Label both axes and give the units (Hz, dB, seconds, BPM). An unlabelled axis makes a plot almost meaningless.
  • Legend and scale: If you show more than one series, add a legend. Explain tick values and any colour scale, and do not rely on colour alone to carry meaning.
  • Readability: Use font sizes large enough to read at the size the figure will actually appear, and keep the design uncluttered.
  • Reference and credit: Refer to each figure in the text (“Figure 3 shows…”) so the reader knows when to look, and cite the source if the figure is not your own.
  • A spectrogram is not a screenshot: Crop to the plot and keep both axis scales. The caption states the sound, the window size, whether the frequency axis is linear or logarithmic, and what the colour scale measures, and then points at the thing to look at with its frequency or time (“the second harmonic at 880 Hz fades after 1.2 s”). Figures labelled only “Linear” and “Log”, or placed at the end without being mentioned, were the most common weakness in two years of spectrogram assignments.
  • The sound itself: For every recording you analyse, give its author, a link, and its licence, as you would for any other source.

The plots produced by the code examples in this book aim to follow these habits: labelled axes with units, and a caption that explains the content.

Referencing and source use¶

Avoid too many direct quotes in your writing. It is better to show that you can reformulate other people’s thinking in your own words. Use page numbers when you cite or paraphrase concrete things in other people’s texts.

The reference list contains exactly the works cited in the text, and nothing else. Cite the chapter or page of a book, give the published source rather than a ResearchGate copy or a page of this book’s website, and cite this textbook once, as a book, not each week’s page as a separate work.

There is no particular reference style asked for in this course. The most important is that you are consistent. Choose one (e.g., APA, Chicago, or Harvard) and stick to it. Use a reference manager (for example, Zotero) to keep track of your references. That will save you a lot of time later. In addition to helping with your references, it can serve as the start of your digital research library.

Use relevant databases for finding literature. Google Scholar is often the first place to start looking, but the university library is a better place to go for getting access to material behind paywalls.

Before you submit¶

Three things decide whether a good text is read as one: that it answers the question that was asked and not a neighbouring one, that it has been revised, and that it arrives in the right form. Check the first against the brief, since every year a few essays answer a different question well.

When you revise, use separate passes so you do not try to fix everything at once:

  • Structure: does the argument flow from paragraph to paragraph?
  • Claims and evidence: is each main point supported?
  • Language and references: clarity, tone, and accurate citation.

Remember to format the text so that it looks coherent and is easy to follow. Use consistent font sizes, heading levels, indents, and spacings. Deliver nice-looking PDFs if you are not asked for something else.

A few practical habits save marks that have nothing to do with understanding. Deliver a PDF unless asked for something else, named with the course code and the assignment number. Open every link you include in a private browser window before you submit; a Freesound upload that is still waiting for moderation is a dead link for the reader, and so is a link to your profile page or to the moderation queue. Link to the sound’s own page, pasted as plain text, and say what the file is: format, duration, device, where the microphone was, and any edits. Attach images, not the session file of the program that made them. Hand in on time.

If an AI tool helped with the text or the analysis, say so where the rules ask for it, and keep the responsibility for every claim.

Spectrograms¶

Reading a spectrogram¶

A spectrogram is a picture of sound. Time runs along the horizontal axis, frequency runs up the vertical axis, and brightness shows how much energy the sound carries at each point. A bright patch means strong energy at that frequency and that moment, while a dark patch means little or none. This part collects the essentials for reading such pictures and for making them, a skill the assignments and the exam both test.

The axes, slowly¶

It is worth being very precise about what each axis means, because a spectrogram is easy to misread.

The horizontal axis is time, exactly as in a waveform display or a timeline. Reading a spectrogram from left to right means moving through the recording from beginning to end. A vertical slice of the image is one moment of sound.

The vertical axis is frequency, measured in hertz. It is a physical quantity, the number of vibrations per second, not a note name. Low frequencies sit at the bottom, high frequencies at the top. The axis can be drawn in two ways. On a linear scale, the distance from 0 to 1,000 Hz is the same as from 1,000 to 2,000 Hz; this is the mathematical default, but it squeezes the musically important low range into a thin band at the bottom. On a logarithmic scale, every octave (doubling of frequency) gets the same amount of space, which matches how pitch works musically: the step from 220 to 440 Hz looks as big as the step from 440 to 880 Hz. Always check which scale you are looking at before interpreting anything.

The third dimension is colour or brightness, which shows how much energy the sound carries at that time and that frequency, usually on a decibel scale. A spectrogram is really a three-dimensional surface (time, frequency, energy) seen from above, with the height of the surface turned into colour. So a bright spot at two seconds and 440 Hz means: at that moment, the recording contained strong vibration at 440 cycles per second. Nothing more, nothing less.

A waveform display shows two of these dimensions (time and overall amplitude). The spectrogram unfolds the amplitude into its frequency content, which is why it shows so much more, and why it takes more care to read.

One note is many lines¶

The most common misreading is to treat a spectrogram as if it were a score or a piano roll. It is not, and the difference matters.

When a single voice or instrument plays a single note, the spectrogram does not show one line. It shows a whole stack of horizontal lines: the partials of the tone, at whole-number multiples of the fundamental frequency (see the terminology in acoustics). Play one note at 220 Hz on a guitar and you will see lines at 220, 440, 660, 880 Hz and upwards. To an eye trained on piano rolls, that stack looks like a large chord. It is one note. The pitch you hear corresponds to the lowest line (which is also the spacing between the lines), and everything above it shapes the timbre of the tone, not extra notes.

This is because a spectrogram shows the physical, composite structure of sound, while a note is a perceptual and cultural unit. The auditory system fuses the whole harmonic stack into a single heard tone; the spectrogram leaves it unfused for you to inspect. Two practical consequences follow. First, to find the melody in a spectrogram, follow the lowest strong line of a stack (or the spacing of the stack), not every line. Second, when several instruments play together, their stacks interleave, and untangling which line belongs to which source becomes genuinely difficult. That difficulty is not a flaw in the picture; it is the same problem your auditory system solves in auditory scene analysis (psychoacoustics), and the reason automatic music transcription remains hard (machine listening).

A quick test when you are unsure whether a stack is one note or a chord: check the spacing. The partials of a single harmonic tone are equally spaced in hertz (220, 440, 660: always 220 apart). The fundamentals of a chord are not; a major triad on A has fundamentals at 220, 277, and 330 Hz, and each of those carries its own stack above it.

What common sounds look like¶

A harmonic sound, such as a sung vowel or a plucked guitar string, appears as a stack of horizontal lines. The lowest line is the fundamental frequency, which normally matches the pitch you hear, and the lines above it are harmonics at whole-number multiples of the fundamental.

A drum hit or click appears as a thin vertical stripe. The event is short in time but spreads its energy across many frequencies at once, so it paints the whole height of the image for an instant.

A voice or instrument with vibrato shows the familiar harmonic stack, but each line waves gently up and down. The waves trace the periodic pitch variation, and they are easiest to spot in the upper harmonics, where the movement is magnified.

Noise, such as a cymbal wash, wind, or hiss, appears as a diffuse wash of energy without clear lines, because the energy is spread continuously across the frequency range.

Silence appears as darkness. In practice a recording is rarely completely dark, since background noise usually leaves a faint glow near the bottom of the image.

The figure below shows all of these in a single synthesised clip.

# Annotated spectrogram of a synthesised clip with a count-in click,
# a harmonic tone with vibrato, a noise burst, and silence
import numpy as np
import librosa
import librosa.display
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.rcParams.update({
    "font.size": 10,
    "axes.titlesize": 11,
    "axes.labelsize": 10,
    "xtick.labelsize": 9,
    "ytick.labelsize": 9,
    "legend.fontsize": 9,
    "figure.figsize": (10, 4),
    "figure.dpi": 100,
    "savefig.dpi": 150,
    "axes.spines.top": False,
    "axes.spines.right": False,
})

rng = np.random.default_rng(2640)
sr = 22050
duration = 6.0
n = int(sr * duration)
t = np.linspace(0, duration, n, endpoint=False)
y = np.zeros(n)

# Count-in clicks: two short broadband bursts
for start in (0.4, 0.9):
    idx = (t >= start) & (t < start + 0.012)
    y[idx] += 0.9 * rng.uniform(-1, 1, idx.sum())

# Sustained harmonic tone with vibrato (f0 = 220 Hz, 5.5 Hz vibrato)
f0, vib_rate, vib_depth = 220.0, 5.5, 8.0
inst_f0 = f0 + vib_depth * np.sin(2 * np.pi * vib_rate * t)
phase0 = 2 * np.pi * np.cumsum(inst_f0) / sr
tone = np.zeros(n)
for k in range(1, 7):
    tone += (0.5 / k) * np.sin(k * phase0)
env = np.where((t >= 1.5) & (t < 3.8), 1.0, 0.0)
ramp = int(0.05 * sr)
attack = np.searchsorted(t, 1.5)
release = np.searchsorted(t, 3.8)
env[attack:attack + ramp] *= np.linspace(0, 1, ramp)
env[release - ramp:release] *= np.linspace(1, 0, ramp)
y += 0.6 * tone * env

# Noise burst
idx = (t >= 4.2) & (t < 4.8)
y[idx] += 0.35 * rng.uniform(-1, 1, idx.sum())

# Silence from 4.8 s onwards (nothing added)
y = y / np.max(np.abs(y))

n_fft, hop_length = 2048, 256
D = librosa.stft(y, n_fft=n_fft, hop_length=hop_length, window='hann')
S_db = librosa.amplitude_to_db(np.abs(D), ref=np.max)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4.2), constrained_layout=True)
img = librosa.display.specshow(S_db, sr=sr, hop_length=hop_length,
                               x_axis='time', y_axis='hz', cmap='magma', ax=ax)
ax.set_ylim(0, 2500)
ax.set_title('An annotated spectrogram of a six-second synthesised clip')
fig.colorbar(img, ax=ax, format='%+2.0f dB')

notes = [
    ('count-in clicks:\nvertical stripes', (0.65, 1900), (1.15, 2300)),
    ('harmonics:\nhorizontal stack', (2.6, 480), (2.05, 2050)),
    ('vibrato:\nwavy lines', (3.3, 1310), (3.85, 1850)),
    ('noise burst:\nbroadband wash', (4.5, 1500), (4.35, 2250)),
    ('silence:\ndarkness', (5.5, 900), (5.15, 1500)),
]
for text, xy, xytext in notes:
    ax.annotate(text, xy=xy, xytext=xytext, color='white',
                ha='center', va='center',
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->', color='white', lw=1.2))
plt.show()

The window-size trade-off¶

Every spectrogram is computed with an analysis window, and the window size sets a trade-off: a short window is sharp in time but blurry in frequency, while a long window is sharp in frequency but blurry in time. The acoustics chapter explains this trade-off in more detail and shows the same signal analysed with three window sizes.

Checklist when describing a spectrogram¶

  • Start with the axes: note the time span, the frequency range, whether the frequency and intensity scales are linear or logarithmic, and how wide the colour scale is (the dynamic range).
  • Name the settings: the window size (in samples or milliseconds) and the overlap, since they decide what the picture can and cannot show.
  • Identify the obvious events: where does something clearly begin, end, or change?
  • Sort the content into harmonic (horizontal lines), percussive (vertical stripes), and noisy (diffuse washes).
  • Describe how the sound develops over time: what stays constant and what moves?
  • State what you cannot conclude: a spectrogram shows physical energy, not perceived loudness, and it does not by itself reveal which instrument or source produced the sound.

Making spectrograms in Sonic Visualiser¶

Sonic Visualiser is the tool used for spectrogram work in this course. Open an audio file (File > Open) and you first see a waveform. Spectrograms are added as layers on top: open the Layer menu and you will find three different spectrogram types. All three are computed from the same analysis; they differ in what they choose to show.

The regular spectrogram¶

Layer > Add Spectrogram (shortcut G) gives the standard, general-purpose view: all frequencies from 0 Hz to half the sampling rate, with energy as colour. This is the view to start from. It shows everything at once: harmonic stacks, percussive stripes, and noise washes. Its weakness is the linear frequency default, which devotes most of the image to high frequencies where relatively little music happens.

The properties pane on the right edge is where the important choices live. Window sets the analysis window size and with it the time–frequency trade-off described in acoustics: 1024 samples is a reasonable general default, longer (2048 or 4096) for sustained tones and fine pitch detail, shorter (256 or 512) for drum hits and onsets. Scale switches the colour mapping (dB is usually the most readable). The frequency axis can be switched between linear and logarithmic, and vertical zoom (the wheels at the bottom right of the pane) lets you concentrate on the range that matters.

The melodic range spectrogram¶

Layer > Add Melodic Range Spectrogram (shortcut M) is a preset of the same analysis tuned for following melody. It restricts the view to a melodic frequency range, uses a logarithmic frequency axis so that octaves get equal space, and uses a longer window for better frequency resolution. Pitch contours, vibrato, and slides read almost like a drawn melodic line. This is usually the best view for questions such as where a melody rises and falls, or how wide a singer’s vibrato is. Remember from the section above that even here, one sung note appears as several parallel lines; the melodic contour is the lowest strong one.

The peak frequency spectrogram¶

Layer > Add Peak Frequency Spectrogram (shortcut K) changes what is drawn. Instead of smearing each component across the width of an analysis bin, it estimates the exact frequency of the strongest component in each bin and draws a thin line there. Harmonic partials become crisp, needle-thin traces whose frequencies can be read off accurately. That makes it the view for questions about tuning, exact harmonic frequencies, or whether two lines are really one. The price is that the picture only means something where there are clear peaks. Noise and reverberation, which really are smeared across frequency, come out strange and speckled.

Which one when¶

For an overview of a whole recording, use the regular spectrogram. To follow pitch and melody, use the melodic range spectrogram. To measure the frequency of a clear tonal sound, use the peak frequency spectrogram. It helps to keep two of them as layers in the same pane and switch between them (the layer tabs at the top of the properties pane): the differences teach you what each view hides.

Exporting a spectrogram as a figure¶

Do not hand in a screenshot of the whole window. Use File > Export Image to save the pane, after switching on the vertical scale and the time ruler in the View menu so that both axes are in the picture, and crop to the plot if you must. The window size, the overlap and the colour scale are not drawn into the image, so write them in the caption. Then treat it as any other figure: a number, a caption that names the sound and the settings, and a sentence in the text that points at what to see.

Studying and the exam¶

Study technique¶

Most people learn more from short focused blocks (~30 minutes) and real breaks than from staring passively at the same page for hours. When you need depth, lean on a calm environment and leave digital distractions aside.

After reading a chapter, try one or more of these:

  • Close the notes and write half a page in your own words.
  • Explain a concept aloud as if to someone new to the topic.
  • Turn each section heading into a question and answer it before you reread.

Since this course is all about listening and experiencing your sonic environments, turn on your senses in everyday life. Try to apply what you learn to what you experience.

Test out AI tools, if you like. Feel free to explore tools such as NotebookLM with this textbook to pose test questions.

Exam preparation¶

This course uses a school exam without any tools. That means that you will need to express yourself without any tools. The questions will not be made to test your memory, but to check whether you have understood the main concepts and how things relate.

The best preparation for the exam is to read through this textbook, show up in class, and do the assignments.

Also practise your general academic writing. This includes sharpening your language, including the use of key terms (for example the difference between “sound” and “audio”).

Last year’s exam showed three things worth practising in advance. Reading spectrograms was the weakest part: use the checklist in the spectrogram guide below on four different recordings until the axes, the scales and the window size are the first things you name. Schafer’s three terms were mixed up: keep the one-line test from the listening chapter in your head. And the question that asked what a study could have done differently lost the most marks: practise the five levers above on one article a week, and always propose one concrete change rather than a general complaint.

During the exam, answer every sub-question, in the order asked, with its letter as a label. A blank sub-question costs more than any wrong answer; in recent years every script in the lowest grades had a part under two hundred words or a sub-question left out, and the strongest wrote 500 to 700 words per part. If time is short, write three sentences for each sub-question first, then go back and expand. If a question freezes you, begin with what you do know and return later.

Two recurring exam questions reward a fixed procedure. For a spectrogram, go through channels, frequency axis (linear or logarithmic, range, unit), time axis (present or not, duration), colour map and decibel scale, and only then events; write each as an observation with its number, then infer the source and name the feature that supports it, then say which setting you would change to test the guess. For the question of why machines struggle to hear as we do, answer from psychoacoustics, not from artificial intelligence: the ear is non-linear, with equal-loudness contours, masking, critical bands, and just noticeable differences, and a machine has none of that unless it is built in.
